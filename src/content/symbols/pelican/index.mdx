A pelican standing over her nest, head bowed to her own chest, turns up
on tabernacle doors, altar fronts, and stained glass with no further
explanation needed in a Catholic church - though the bird in the picture
has nothing to do with how a real pelican behaves. The legend behind it
is older than most Christian art still using it.

## A legend older than the churches that use it

  src={merlinStainedGlass}

The story comes from the Physiologus, a Greek compilation of animal
lore written for Christian teaching somewhere between the 2nd and 4th
centuries, long before any of the stained glass it inspired. In its
account, the mother pelican's young strike at her, and in the ensuing
conflict they end up lifeless. After three days of mourning, she wounds
her own side and lets her blood fall over their bodies, restoring them
to life. By the time this story reached the
medieval Latin bestiaries that spread it across Europe, it had simplified
into the gentler version still told today: a mother feeding her hungry
young with her own blood when no other food can be found.

## Why the Church kept it anyway

<InlineImage
  src={pisanelloMedal}
  alt="Bronze medal in relief showing a pelican standing over three chicks, piercing her own breast"
  caption="Pelican in Her Piety, reverse of a bronze medal by Pisanello, c. 1446. National Gallery of Art, Washington."
  credit="Pisanello, Wikimedia Commons, CC0"
  side="left"
/>

No pelican actually does this. The real birds sometimes press their
heavy bills against their chests to rest them, and some species take on
a reddish flush around the throat pouch during breeding season - enough,
probably, to start a rumor, but nothing like self-wounding. That was
never the point of the Physiologus, though; it wasn't trying to describe
birds accurately, it was looking for animals whose supposed habits could
teach a Christian lesson. A parent who restores life to its young at the
cost of its own blood was too good a match for the Crucifixion and the
Eucharist to set aside just because it wasn't true to life.

Thomas Aquinas, the 13th-century Dominican theologian, picked up the image directly, writing
"Pie Pelicane, Jesu Domine" - "Good pelican, Lord Jesus" - in his
Eucharistic hymn Adoro Te Devote, asking to be washed clean in Christ's
blood the way the legend's chicks were restored by their mother's. That
line is still sung at Benediction, the Eucharistic devotion, in some
parishes today. The bird itself never left church architecture: it's one of the most recognizable
Eucharistic images carved into altars and set into windows, a reminder
that a symbol's staying power in devotional art has very little to do
with whether the animal behind it checks out.

The image even made a jump no one in the medieval Church could have
anticipated: it's the bird on the flag and seal of Louisiana, put there
by a territory with deep French and Spanish Catholic roots, where the
same self-sacrificing mother pelican had already been a familiar
devotional image for generations before it became a civic one. The
state kept the posture and the wound exactly as the bestiaries drew it -
proof that a good symbol can leave the sanctuary entirely and still be
recognized for what it originally meant.
