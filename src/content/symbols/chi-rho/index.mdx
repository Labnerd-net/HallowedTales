Two letters laid one over the other - a sideways X crossed by a P - turn up
on Easter candles, church vestments, old tombstones, and Vatican
liturgical items. They're the first two letters of "Christos" in Greek,
chi and rho, combined into a single mark. The story most people know about
how it became a Christian symbol involves an emperor, a battle, and a
vision in the sky.

## A vision before the battle

In 312, Constantine marched on Rome to face his rival Maxentius at the
Milvian Bridge. According to Lactantius, a Christian writer composing his
account within a year or two of the battle, Constantine was told in a
dream the night before to mark his soldiers' shields with "the heavenly
sign of God" - a shape Lactantius describes plainly as an X crossed by a
line bent round at the top, the Chi-Rho.

A generation later, Eusebius of Caesarea, the church historian, told a more dramatic version.
Writing after Constantine's death, and claiming to relate what the emperor
told him personally under oath, Eusebius has Constantine see a cross of
light in the sky at midday, with the words "in this sign, conquer" (in hoc
signo vinces), followed that night by a dream of Christ instructing him to
adopt the sign as his standard. It's Eusebius's account that produced the
famous motto, even though it's the later and more embellished of the two.

Constantine won the battle and became sole emperor in the West. Whichever
version of the vision is closer to what actually happened in his own
mind, he adopted the Chi-Rho as a military standard called the labarum,
which began appearing on his coinage within a few years.

## A symbol older than the vision

What's easy to miss in the vision story is that the Chi-Rho didn't
originate with Constantine at all. Christians were already using it as an
abbreviation for "Christos" before 312, including as a mark in catacomb
burials - closer to a secret identifier than the triumphant battle
standard it was about to become. Constantine's contribution wasn't
inventing the symbol; it was making it official, visible, and eventually
safe to display in public, now backed by the authority of the empire
rather than whispered among the persecuted.

## From battle standard to church art

Within a few generations, the symbol moved from military insignia into
the Church's own visual language, and the shift is visible on the
sarcophagi: 4th- and 5th-century Christian tombs carve the Chi-Rho inside
a laurel wreath - the same wreath that had crowned Roman military
victories for centuries - now flanked by Alpha and Omega, the first and
last letters of the Greek alphabet, standing in for Christ as "the
beginning and the end." A Roman symbol of triumph becomes a declaration
of the Resurrection.

The same combination - a face, a Chi-Rho, nothing else needed to identify
the subject as Christian - turns up as far from Rome as Roman Britain, on
a mosaic floor excavated at Hinton St Mary in Dorset. Whether the bust at
its center is meant to be Christ or the emperor who made this symbol safe
to display is still debated; either reading makes the same point about how
completely the Chi-Rho had become shorthand for "Christian" by the 4th
century.

## Still on the candle today

The Chi-Rho never really left. It's a customary feature of the Paschal
candle lit every year at the Easter Vigil, usually inscribed alongside a
cross and the Alpha and Omega, and it still shows up in church heraldry,
vestments, and altar linens across the Catholic world. It's also easy to
confuse with a different, later Christian monogram, the Jesuit "IHS,"
short for Iesus rather than Christos. The two get used almost
interchangeably in casual conversation, but they trace back to different
words, different alphabets, and different centuries.
