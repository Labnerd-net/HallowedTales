Franciscans today are often easiest to spot not by a crucifix but by a
small T-shaped cross, worn as a pendant or pinned to a lapel. It's the
Tau - the nineteenth letter of the Greek alphabet, but more importantly
here, the last letter of the Hebrew alphabet - and Francis of Assisi
made it his personal mark centuries before it became the order's.

The Tau's biblical credentials go back to the prophet Ezekiel, who
describes God's angel marking the foreheads of the faithful with a
"tav" - in the old Hebrew script still in use when Ezekiel was written,
a letter shaped like a cross or an X - sparing them from the destruction
about to fall on Jerusalem. Christian readers took that mark as an
early sign of the cross of Christ, and Pope Innocent III preached on
exactly that connection at the opening of the Fourth Lateran Council in
1215, calling on the faithful to let the Tau be written on their hearts
through penance.

Francis of Assisi was at that council. Franciscan tradition holds that
the sermon struck him deeply enough to shape how he signed his name for
the rest of his life, though the surviving record only shows us the
result, not his reaction in the moment.

## A signature that survives

What does survive, and survives in Francis's own hand, is the Chartula -
a small parchment he wrote for Brother Leo in 1224, carrying a blessing
and a set of praises to God. At the foot of the text, Francis drew a
Tau. Brother Leo, who kept the parchment, added his own note confirming
that Francis had written it himself. It's one of only a few documents
that survive from Francis's own pen, which makes this small drawn letter
unusually solid evidence for something that could otherwise have been
just a later story attached to a famous saint: Francis really did use
the Tau as his own mark, on a real document that still exists.

## From one friar's signature to an order's symbol

The Tau outgrew Francis's own signature fairly quickly. He's said to have
had it painted on the walls and doors of places where his friars stayed,
and the letter's plain shape - two strokes, no ornament - made it easy to
reproduce on building stone, wax seals, and later, the badges worn by lay
Franciscan tertiaries who wanted a visible sign of the Rule they'd taken
up without formally entering religious life. That practical, unadorned
quality is still the Tau's advantage today: easy to wear, easy to draw,
and still carrying the same sign Francis is said to have heard preached
at the Lateran eight centuries ago.
