A heart wrapped in thorns, topped by a small flame or cross, shows up on
holy cards, home altars, and parish names across the Catholic world. It's
one of the few major devotional images with a traceable birthday: a
French convent, a nun named Margaret Mary Alacoque, and a series of
visions she reported over about eighteen months in the 1670s.

## Eighteen months of visions

Margaret Mary Alacoque was a Visitation nun at the convent in
Paray-le-Monial, in Burgundy, when she began describing a series of
visions starting in December 1673. In the first, she said, Christ let
her rest her head against his heart and showed her "the wonders of his
love." Further visions over the following months returned to the same
image, with Christ expressing sorrow at how little that love was
returned. The best known of them, which she called the Great Apparition,
came in June 1675, when Christ asked that the Friday after the feast of
Corpus Christi be set aside each year as a feast specifically honoring
his heart.

## A hard sell at home

Margaret Mary's account met resistance before it met acceptance - her
own Visitation sisters weren't convinced, and it took the backing of her
confessor, the Jesuit priest Claude La Colombiere, to keep the devotion
alive at all. She didn't write her own account down until roughly a
decade later, around 1685, at the instruction of a later confessor,
Fr. Joseph Rolin. The devotion spent about 75 years spreading informally
through Jesuit networks and local practice before Rome gave it universal
status. Pope Pius IX extended the Feast of the Sacred Heart to the whole
Church only in 1856, and Margaret Mary herself wasn't canonized until
1920. That gap is a reminder of how slowly even an eventually mainstream
devotion can move through the Church's own approval process.

## An old image, a new occasion

Margaret Mary didn't invent the underlying image from nothing. Medieval
mystics, including Gertrude of Helfta and Mechthild of Hackeborn, had
already written about Christ's heart as a symbol of divine love
centuries earlier. What her visions supplied was an occasion - a
specific feast, a specific promise attached to receiving Communion on
nine consecutive First Fridays - that turned a mystical image already
circulating in religious writing into an organized, popular devotion
with its own art and its own day on the calendar. Pompeo Batoni's 1767
altarpiece for the Church of the Gesu in Rome, painted less than a
century after Margaret Mary's death, fixed the now-familiar visual
formula - the exposed heart, the ring of thorns, the small cross or flame
above it - that's been copied onto holy cards and home shrines ever
since.
