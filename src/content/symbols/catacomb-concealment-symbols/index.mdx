Walk through a Roman catacomb and most of what's carved into the walls
looks, at a glance, like ordinary Roman decoration: a ship's anchor, a
bird, a peacock showing off its tail. None of it would have alarmed anyone
searching for Christians. That was rather the point. Alongside the fish
and its hidden acrostic, three more everyday images did double duty for
early believers - familiar enough to pass unnoticed, meaningful enough to
be worth repeating on hundreds of tombs.

## The anchor: a cross that doesn't look like one

The anchor is one of the most common images in the Roman catacombs - by
some counts, close to seventy survive in the Catacomb of Priscilla alone.
Its scriptural anchor, so to speak, is Hebrews 6:19: Christian hope
described as "an anchor for the soul, firm and secure." But the shape
carried a second layer, too. Turn an anchor's crossbar and shank the right
way and it traces a cross - a sailor's tool standing in for the instrument
of the Crucifixion at a time when the cross itself was rarely shown
outright. The funerary stele of a woman named Licinia Amias, carved in the
early 3rd century and recovered near the old Vatican necropolis, carries
exactly this combination: an anchor beside a fish, under a Greek
inscription reading "fish of the living."

A dove needed even less disguising - it was already a common Roman image
for peace and a settled household. Christians painted it over baptism
scenes as a figure for the Holy Spirit, recalling the dove that descended
at Jesus's own baptism in the Jordan, and set it alone on tombs as a
simpler wish for the peace of the soul. It also pulled in an older story:
Noah's dove, sent out from the ark and returning with an olive branch as
proof the flood had ended. Read that way, the ark became an image of the
Church, the floodwaters a figure for baptism, and the dove's return a
promise of peace on the other side of it - the same associative logic
that let the fish carry baptismal meaning.

## The peacock: a bird that (almost) doesn't rot

  src={nahariyaPeacocks}

The peacock's Christian meaning rests on a claim about its body: that its
flesh doesn't decay. Augustine took the claim seriously enough to test it
himself. In the City of God, he describes being served peacock at dinner,
setting aside a piece of the meat, and checking on it a month later, then
again after a year - each time finding it merely dried out, never
putrefied. "Who but God the Creator of all things," he asks, "has given to
the flesh of the peacock its antiseptic property?" Whatever the biology,
the belief made the bird a natural image for the resurrection and for a
body made incorruptible - which is why peacocks turn up carved on
sarcophagus lids and set into church floor mosaics, often in pairs
flanking a vase or cup, long after the catacombs themselves had gone out
of use.

## Hidden in plain sight, not in code

None of these three images required a Christian to invent a new picture
from nothing - that was the advantage. An anchor could be a sailor's
marker, a dove a wish for peace, a peacock simple decoration, and in most
cases probably was exactly that for most people who saw them. The
Christian meaning layered on top rather than replacing the ordinary one,
which is a quieter kind of survival than a secret handshake: no one
outside the faith needed to notice anything unusual at all.
