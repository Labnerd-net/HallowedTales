Luminosity, sometimes called a photism in the older literature, is a light
reported shining from or around a saint during deep prayer or ecstasy.
Francis of Assisi, the 13th-century friar who founded the order that bears
his name, is remembered for exactly this. By the accounts of the brothers
who shared his life at the friary, they would sometimes wake in the night
to find light spilling from the cracks of Francis's cell, bright enough to
draw them out of bed to look, only to find him alone at prayer with no
lamp or candle lit.

The brothers who reported it treated it less as a spectacle than as a
quiet sign of how completely Francis's prayer absorbed him. Thomas of
Celano, who compiled the earliest biography of Francis at the Church's
request not long after his death, folded the detail in among the rest of
a life already full of reported wonders, from preaching to birds to
bearing the stigmata in his own body.

## A phenomenon with centuries of company

Francis's case anchors this category because it's one of the
earliest and most frequently retold, but the pattern shows up again and
again in the centuries after him. Padre Pio and Teresa of Avila both carry
similar reported accounts of light during prayer. Philip Neri is described
as having been seen glowing during Mass. Martin de Porres and John Bosco
round out the pattern further, each reportedly observed by the people
around them rather than by any outside examination - a phenomenon told
through the eyes of witnesses, century after century.
