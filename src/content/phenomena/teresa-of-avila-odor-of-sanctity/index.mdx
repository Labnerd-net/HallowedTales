The odor of sanctity is a fragrance, with no natural source, reported
around a saint's body or relics. Teresa of Avila, the 16th-century
Carmelite reformer and mystic later named a Doctor of the Church, is one
of the tradition's most frequently cited cases. By the accounts of the
sisters and officials present when her tomb was opened nine months after
her death, in keeping with the order's custom of translating a founder's
remains, those gathered reported a lily-like fragrance filling the space,
with no flowers or perfume anywhere nearby to explain it.

The detail was treated by her community less as a curiosity than as a
quiet confirmation of a life they already regarded as remarkable - Teresa
had spent her later years reforming the Carmelite order and writing on
prayer in a voice still read today. The fragrance became part of how her
story was told and retold in the centuries that followed, alongside the
rest of her reported mystical experience.

## A phenomenon with centuries of company

Teresa's case anchors this category because it's one of the most
frequently told, but she's far from the only one. John Bosco carries a
similar reported account from his own death. Padre Pio's stigmata wounds
were reportedly noted by those close to him for smelling of violets,
rather than the way an ordinary wound might, a detail his community saw as
part of the same pattern. Gerard Majella's case rounds out the tradition
further - each one a report passed down by the people present, not a
claim tested by anyone outside that circle.
