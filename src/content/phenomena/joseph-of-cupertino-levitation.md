Few saints have a reported phenomenon as frequently or as vividly
documented as Joseph of Cupertino's levitation. By the accounts gathered
during his lifetime and after, Joseph - a 17th-century Franciscan friar
known for his clumsiness and simplicity as much as his devotion - would
rise into the air during moments of intense prayer or at Mass, sometimes
drifting toward a statue or altar, sometimes rising high enough that
witnesses described needing to pull him back down by his habit.

The incidents reportedly happened often enough, and in front of enough
witnesses, including visiting dignitaries, that his superiors eventually
restricted him from celebrating public Mass or appearing before crowds,
out of concern for the disruption it caused. He became known, even within
his own lifetime, by the nickname "the Flying Friar."

## A pattern, not a single story

Joseph's case anchors this category because it's the most frequently
reported example of levitation in Catholic tradition, but he's far from
alone. Teresa of Avila, John of the Cross, and Francis of Assisi all carry
similar reported incidents, and Philip Neri is said to have been found, on
at least one occasion, caught up into the branches of a tree. Taken
together, the pattern says less about any one saint and more about how
consistently this specific image - a person lifted off the ground by the
force of their own prayer - recurs across very different lives and
centuries.
