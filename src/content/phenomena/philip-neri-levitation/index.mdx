Philip Neri was born in Florence in 1515 and spent most of his life in
Rome, where he became known as the city's "apostle." He gathered young
people, pilgrims, and the poor into informal evenings of prayer, music, and
conversation that grew into the Oratory. He was also famous for his
humor. He is remembered for playful pranks meant to keep himself and his
friends from taking themselves too seriously, and for insisting that a
cheerful heart was no obstacle to holiness.

That is part of what makes the stories about him so striking. By the
accounts of his early biographers, the same man who joked in the streets
could be caught up into prayer so deeply that he rose from the ground.
Witnesses described him lifted a little above the floor while celebrating
Mass or praying alone. Those who stumbled on it reportedly kept quiet out
of respect, while Philip himself tried to hide it and turn the talk
elsewhere.

## A heart too full

The accounts also tell of a night in 1544, when Philip was praying in the
catacombs of San Sebastiano, and he felt his heart enlarged by a rush of
love. His biographers say that from then on his heart would race when he
prayed, to the point that his companions could feel it, and that he often
seemed to glow with warmth in the cold. For many readers, this is the
center of his story: not a feat, but a joy too large to stay inside him.

## A pattern with company

Levitation is reported of many saints. Joseph of Cupertino, the 17th-century
"Flying Friar," is the best known, and Teresa of Avila, the Carmelite
reformer, along with her friend John of the Cross and Francis of Assisi,
the founder of the Franciscans, carry similar accounts. Philip's version
is remembered for its gentleness: a man whose joy in prayer was so large
that it lifted him, and who was happier laughing with his friends than
being watched.
