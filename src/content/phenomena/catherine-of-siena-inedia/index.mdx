Inedia is the reported ability to live for long stretches on little or no
ordinary food. Catherine of Siena, a 14th-century Dominican tertiary who
would go on to be named a Doctor of the Church, is remembered as one of the
earliest and most closely documented cases. By the accounts of those
closest to her, she ate almost nothing in her later years beyond the
Eucharist, to the point that her own confessor and the women she lived
among grew worried and pressed her to eat more.

Catherine herself reportedly described the practice less as a feat and more
as something she couldn't help - that ordinary food had become difficult
for her body to take in at all. Her confessor, Raymond of Capua, recorded
the pattern in the biography he wrote after her death, treating it as one
thread in a life otherwise devoted to nursing plague victims, writing
letters that shaped Church politics of her day, and talking a pope into
returning to Rome.

## A phenomenon with centuries of company

Catherine's case anchors this category because it's among the earliest
well-recorded examples, but the pattern recurs across the centuries that
follow. Nicholas of Flue reportedly went nineteen years without ordinary
food. Lidwina of Schiedam and Rose of Lima carry similar reported accounts
from the medieval and colonial periods. The pattern continues into the
20th century with cases like Therese Neumann, Marthe Robin, Alexandrina da
Costa, and Luisa Piccarreta - more recent enough that physicians were
sometimes brought in at the time to observe and record what they saw,
even though none of these cases carry formal Vatican verification as a
miracle.
