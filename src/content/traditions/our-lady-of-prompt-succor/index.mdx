In March 1809, a French Ursuline nun named Mother St. Michel Gensoul wrote a letter asking Pope Pius VII for permission to leave France and join a struggling Ursuline mission in New Orleans. It was not an easy request to grant. Napoleon's troops held much of Italy at the time, and the pope himself was effectively a prisoner of the French emperor, with little freedom to conduct the ordinary business of his office. Gensoul prayed before a statue of the Virgin Mary as she sent the letter, promising that if she received a prompt and favorable reply, she would see Mary honored in New Orleans under the title Our Lady of Prompt Succor. The pope's answer came back just over a month later, on April 29, 1809, granting exactly what she had asked.

True to her vow, Gensoul commissioned a new statue once she had permission to travel: Mary holding the child Jesus, carved with robes rendered in a way meant to suggest swift, hurrying movement, fitting the title she had promised. She arrived in New Orleans on the last day of 1810, bringing the statue to the Old Ursuline Convent on Chartres Street in the city's French Quarter.

Two years later, in 1812, a major fire swept through the French Quarter, and the convent stood in its path. According to the story told within the Ursuline community ever since, a sister named St. Anthony set the small statue on a windowsill as Mother St. Michel prayed aloud, asking Mary to hasten to their aid. The wind reportedly shifted, carrying the flames away from the convent, which survived largely untouched while buildings nearby burned. No outside account of the fire corroborates this version of events; it survives only through the sisters' own retelling.

The devotion's defining moment came a few years after that. On the morning of January 8, 1815, with a British invasion force bearing down on New Orleans and American troops significantly outnumbered, a priest named Louis William DuBourg offered Mass before the statue at the Ursuline chapel. During that same Mass, the convent's prioress, Mother Ste. Marie Olivier de Vezin, made a vow of her own: an annual Mass of Thanksgiving, sung every year afterward, if the Americans were spared heavy losses that day. According to the convent's tradition, a courier burst into the chapel with news of an American victory while the Mass was still being said. General Andrew Jackson, who led the American defense, showed real gratitude toward the sisters afterward and is known to have visited them on later trips to the city. The Mass vowed that day has been kept every January 8 since, long after the battle itself passed into American history as one of the most lopsided victories of the war.

Rome took formal notice of the devotion as the century went on. Pope Pius IX authorized public veneration and fixed January 8 as the statue's feast day in 1851. Four decades later, Pope Leo XIII approved a full Canonical Coronation; New Orleans Catholics donated the gold and gems themselves, and Archbishop Francis Janssens carried out the rite in 1895. Leo XIII returned to the devotion again in 1897, raising its confraternity to the rank of Archconfraternity. The clearest sign of how far it had traveled beyond one convent chapel came in 1928, when Pope Pius XI, acting on a petition from New Orleans' own archbishop and two other Louisiana bishops, declared Our Lady of Prompt Succor the principal patroness of the entire state of Louisiana and of the Archdiocese of New Orleans.

The original statue Gensoul brought across the Atlantic in 1810 is still venerated in New Orleans today, now at what is formally recognized as the National Shrine of Our Lady of Prompt Succor. The title itself, born out of one nun's hurried, uncertain letter to a pope with no real power of his own to grant it, has outlasted the particular crisis that produced it by more than two centuries.
