Ask around about why Catholics dedicate the month of May to Mary, the
mother of Jesus, and you'll likely hear some version of the same story:
the Church took over an old pagan spring festival - the Roman goddess
Flora, maybe, or the secular folk custom of crowning a village girl "May
Queen" with flowers - and quietly renamed it in Mary's honor. It's a tidy
story. It also doesn't hold up.

The secular May Queen custom is real, and it does have pre-Christian roots
in European spring folklore - garlands, flowers, a crowned girl standing
in for the turning of the season. But nothing in the historical record
connects that folk custom to the Church's Marian devotion. They're two
separate, true things that happened to land in the same month, not one
tradition descended from the other. No document, letter, or decree bridges
them; the resemblance is just a resemblance.

What actually produced "Mary's month" is a much less romantic, much better
documented trail. The earliest thread is a crowning custom, not a May
custom at all: between 731 and 741, Pope Gregory III donated a jeweled
diadem to crown a sacred image at Saint Peter's in Rome, centuries before
anyone tied image-crowning specifically to May. Separately, in 17th- and
18th-century Italy, parishes began experimenting with whole-month Marian
devotion rather than a single feast day - a documented 1739 practice at
Grezzano, near Verona, and a 1747 recommendation from the Archbishop of
Genoa that families keep a Marian devotion at home throughout May.

The version that actually spread worldwide traces to one Jesuit priest.
Toward the end of the 18th century, Father Latomia, teaching at the Roman
College, personally vowed to dedicate the month of May to Mary, hoping the
devotion would counter immorality among his students. The practice caught
on from one Jesuit college to the next, then spread into ordinary parish
life. In 1785, fellow Jesuit Alfonso Muzzarelli published a devotional
manual in Ferrara, *Il mese di Maria* ("The Month of Mary, or May"), laying
out daily readings and prayers for each day of the month - essentially the
structure still used today. Thirty years later, in 1815, Pope Pius VII
granted a partial indulgence for the practice, giving Rome's own stamp of
approval to what had started as one priest's personal vow.

## Why it still matters

May crowning ceremonies, home altars decorated with flowers, parish-wide
daily devotions - all of it traces back not to a borrowed pagan festival
but to a Jesuit schoolteacher trying to shape his students' character, and
a devotional manual that happened to catch on. That's arguably a better
story than the myth: a single, documented act of personal devotion grew,
college by college and parish by parish, into a universal practice still
kept by Catholics every spring.
