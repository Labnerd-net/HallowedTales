When public Catholic worship was banned under Ireland's Penal Laws,
congregations took their Masses outdoors, to hillsides, bogs, and forest
clearings where a flat boulder could stand in for an altar. A priest,
himself breaking the law just by being there, would say Mass on the bare
stone while lookouts kept watch on the surrounding high ground for
soldiers. Hundreds of these sites are still known today as Mass rocks -
*carraig an Aifrinn* in Irish - scattered across the countryside, many
still marked with a small cross or plaque.

The best-documented is at Tomhaggard, County Wexford. Local tradition
holds that on Christmas morning in 1653, Fr. Nicholas Mayler, the parish
priest of Tacumshane and Tomhaggard, was saying Mass at the townland's
Mass rock when Cromwellian soldiers arrived and killed him. A woman in
the congregation, Mrs. Lambert, hid the Mass chalice in her skirts and
later buried it by a nearby lake rather than let it be seized; it
eventually passed back to the parish through the priest's family in the
19th century. That same chalice is still brought out once a year, for
the Christmas Day Mass celebrated at the Tomhaggard Mass rock today.

## Why it still matters

Most Mass rocks don't come with a story as specific as Tomhaggard's -
for the large majority, the only record is a family's memory, written
down generations later, that "the old people" said Mass was once offered
there. But the sites themselves have outlasted the laws that created
them. Several dioceses revived the custom of an annual open-air Mass at
a local Mass rock during the COVID-19 pandemic, when indoor gatherings
were restricted - an unplanned echo, thirteen generations on, of the
exact circumstance in which the custom began.
