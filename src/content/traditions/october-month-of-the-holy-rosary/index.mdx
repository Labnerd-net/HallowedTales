The story is usually told like this: on a morning in October 1571, Pope
Pius V was in a meeting in Rome with his treasurer when he suddenly rose
from his chair, crossed to a window, and gazed east. A moment later he
announced that the Christian fleet had won. No courier, rider, or ship
could possibly have carried the news to Rome that fast - the pope, the
story goes, simply knew.

What's actually on the record is quieter, but still remarkable in its
own way. In the weeks before the battle, Pius V called on Catholics
across Europe to pray the Rosary for the fleet of the Holy League, the
coalition of Christian states assembled to meet the Ottoman navy, and he
ordered the churches of Rome kept open day and night so people could
pray there without pause. The fleets met near the Greek coast at Lepanto
on October 7, 1571, and the Holy League won. But the vision at the
window doesn't appear in any record from the time. It shows up only in
biographies of Pius V written after his death, in part to build
the case for the sainthood he was later granted - and it runs straight
into a documented fact: news of the victory didn't reach Rome until
October 26, nineteen days after the battle.

What Pius V did do, once word finally arrived, was institute a feast of
thanksgiving. In 1572 he named it "Our Lady of Victory" - a title that
credited the win itself, framed as a military outcome Mary had secured.
It took another pope, Gregory XIII, to shift the feast's center of
gravity: in 1573 he renamed it the "Feast of the Holy Rosary," moving
the emphasis away from the battle and onto the Rosary as the means of
intercession behind it. Those are two separate decisions, a year apart,
even though the popular shorthand - "the Rosary won Lepanto" - tends to
flatten them into one.

The feast stayed just that, a single day, for three centuries. October
became a Rosary month because one pope decided it should. In his 1883
encyclical *Supremi Apostolatus Officio*, Pope Leo XIII wrote that he
wanted the whole month of October "consecrated to the Holy Queen of the
Rosary," and asked that five decades of the Rosary, together with the
Litany of Loreto, be recited daily in every parish from October 1
through November 2. He didn't stop there - between 1883 and 1898, Leo
XIII wrote eleven more encyclicals on the Rosary, including *Octobri
Mense* in 1891, making the whole-month devotion one of the most
repeatedly and personally reinforced observances on the Church's
calendar.

## Why it still matters

The Lepanto legend is the part of this story people remember, but it's
not the part that actually built the custom. What made October "Rosary
month" wasn't a pope's uncanny vision in 1571 - it was a pope's plain,
dated, written instruction more than three centuries later, repeated
across a dozen encyclicals until it took. The legend gives the devotion
its drama; Leo XIII's paper trail is what gives it its shape today, in
parishes that still pray the Rosary together every October without
needing the window-gazing story to be true.
