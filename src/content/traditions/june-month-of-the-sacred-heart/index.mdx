At the Visitation convent of Paray-le-Monial, in Burgundy, France, a nun
named Margaret Mary Alacoque experienced a series of visions of Christ
between December 1673 and June 1675. The last and most important of these,
remembered as the Great Apparition, came on June 16, 1675. In it, Christ
asked her to establish a liturgical feast honoring his heart, to be kept on
the Friday after the octave of Corpus Christi - eight days after that
feast - along with two companion
practices: receiving Communion on the first Friday of each month, and
keeping a weekly hour of prayer, the Holy Hour. Margaret Mary's Jesuit
confessor, Claude La Colombière, took her account seriously enough to
authenticate it in writing at the time - which means this devotion rests
on a contemporary record, not a story assembled generations later.

The feast day came relatively quickly, within her own community, but the
custom of dedicating the entire month of June didn't arrive for another
century. It traces to 1790, when a French Jesuit named Alexandre Lanfant
circulated a pamphlet calling for forty days of prayer leading up to a
solemn consecration to the Sacred Heart. That pamphlet is the actual
starting point of the whole-month observance - a separate milestone from
the 1675 apparition, even though the two are often folded together in
casual retellings.

Lanfant didn't have long to see his devotion take hold: he was among the
priests killed in the upheavals of September 1792, and was later
beatified by Pope Pius XI.

The feast itself kept spreading regardless: the Vatican's Congregation
of Rites, which oversaw liturgy, extended it to the entire Latin Church
by decree on August 23, 1856, under Pope Pius IX, and Pope Leo XIII
raised it to first-class rank in 1889.

Leo XIII went further still. A German Visitation nun, Sister Mary of the
Divine Heart (born Maria Droste zu Vischering), wrote to him in June 1898
and again in January 1899, saying Christ had asked her to request a
consecration of the whole world to the Sacred Heart. Leo XIII agreed: he
issued *Annum Sacrum*, signing it on May 25, 1899, and it took effect on
June 11, 1899. Sister Mary didn't live to see it: she died on
June 8, 1899, the Feast of the Sacred Heart that year, just three days before
the world-consecration she had personally asked for actually happened.

## Why it still matters

Few Catholic devotions carry as tight a documentary thread as this one: a
written confessor's account from 1675, a martyred priest's pamphlet from
1790, a Vatican decree from 1856, and a dying nun's request answered three
days after her death in 1899. Each piece stands on its own record rather
than inherited legend. The June devotion that grew out of it - First
Fridays, the Holy Hour, the feast itself - still asks the same thing it
asked at Paray-le-Monial: a little regular, deliberate attention paid to
one specific image of divine love.
