After an Act of Parliament in 1584 made it treason simply to be a Catholic
priest in England, or to knowingly shelter one, Catholic families who kept
priests in their households needed somewhere to put them when the
searchers - known as pursuivants - came knocking. The answer was built
into the houses themselves: small chambers hidden behind false walls,
under stairs, inside chimney breasts, and beneath floorboards, built to
hold a man still and silent for as long as a search might last.

The master of the craft was Nicholas Owen, a Jesuit lay brother so slight
he was nicknamed "Little John." A trained carpenter, Owen spent roughly
eighteen years working alone and mostly at night to avoid even his own
household's suspicion. He built hides into the great recusant houses -
homes of Catholics who refused to attend Anglican services - of
the Midlands and beyond: Harvington Hall, Baddesley Clinton, Oxburgh
Hall, Sawston Hall, and more. Fr. John Gerard, a priest who owed his life
to Owen's work, wrote that "his chief employment was in making of secret
places to hide priests," and that no one else came close to his skill at
both devising and building them. Harvington Hall alone still has seven of
his hides, including one reached through a false fireplace and another
tucked behind a smaller hiding place under a staircase.

## Why it still matters

Owen's own hides eventually held him. After the Gunpowder Plot in 1605,
he and a fellow lay brother hid for four days in one of his own
creations at Hindlip Hall, Worcestershire, with nothing to eat but an
apple, while two priests hid elsewhere in the same house. He may have let
himself be found on purpose, to draw the searchers' attention away from
the others. He was taken to the Tower of London and died there in March
1606, having betrayed no one. He was canonized in 1970 as one of the
Forty Martyrs of England and Wales, the group of English and Welsh
martyrs canonized together.

The hides he and others like him built worked: executions of priests
grew rare by the 1620s, even as the number of priests quietly working
the country nearly tripled - safer hiding meant more priests survived to
keep serving their hidden congregations. Harvington Hall's priest holes are still there to walk through
today, the physical remainder of a craft built entirely around not being
found.
