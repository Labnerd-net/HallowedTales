Meditating on Mary's sorrows - her grief at standing by the Cross as her
son died - is a devotion with roots in the 12th century, taken up by
monastic writers like Saint Anselm of Canterbury and Saint Bernard of
Clairvaux and later spread by the Cistercian monks. It became a defining devotion of the Servite order,
founded in Florence in 1233, whose friars built their whole spiritual
life around keeping Mary company in that moment. By 1692, Pope Innocent
XII had authorized a Feast of the Seven Dolours (Sorrows) for the third Sunday of
September - but only as a local permission, not yet something every
Catholic parish observed.

The feast's real turning point came in 1814, and it came from an
unlikely source: a pope who had just spent years as a prisoner. Pope
Pius VII had been held captive by Napoleon, first at Savona and then at
the palace of Fontainebleau, as Napoleon tried to bend the papacy to his
will. When Napoleon's empire finally collapsed in 1814, Pius VII was
released and returned to Rome. Of all the feasts he might have chosen to
mark his own deliverance, he chose this one - Mary, present and
steadfast through suffering she could not stop. On September 18,
1814, he extended the Feast of the Seven Dolours to the entire Latin
Church. A pope freed from captivity gave the whole Church a feast about
standing faithfully through hardship. Pope Pius X later fixed the date
permanently at September 15, the day after the Exaltation of the Holy
Cross, which is where it remains today.

## Why it still matters

Pius VII's choice gives this devotion a resonance beyond its liturgical
details: it was a thanksgiving offered by someone who had just lived
through his own ordeal. The "whole month" framing that's grown up around
September is looser than that - no pope ever decreed the month itself
the way October was decreed for the Rosary - but it's a natural
extension of a feast with a genuinely strong story behind its date.
