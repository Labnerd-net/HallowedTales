The custom traces back to one abbot, one monastery, and one simple
decision. In 998, Saint Odilo, abbot of the monastery of Cluny in
Burgundy, instituted an annual commemoration for all the faithful
departed, to be kept by his monks on November 2, the day after All
Saints' Day. The idea was straightforward: after honoring the saints in
heaven on November 1, spend the next day in prayer for everyone else who
had died - the ordinary faithful, not yet necessarily perfected, still
on their way. From Cluny, one of the most influential monasteries in
medieval Europe, the practice spread outward over the following
centuries until it became a custom of the wider Church.

Rome gave the devotion its own civic shape in the late Middle Ages.
Confraternities dedicated specifically to praying for the dead and
burying the poor formed in Rome in 1448 and again in 1538 - the Compagnia
della Pietà and, later, the Archconfraternity of Death and Prayer -
turning what had started as a monastic observance into organized lay
charity as well as prayer.

One of the custom's most charming details comes from 1736, when Pope
Clement XII issued a brief - a formal papal letter - granting a
hundred-day indulgence for a
specific evening practice: ringing a bell and reciting prayers for the
dead. It's a small, concrete image - a bell rung at dusk, a household or
parish pausing to remember those who'd died - and it shows how
specifically the Church was willing to attach spiritual benefit to an
ordinary, repeatable act of remembrance.

The devotion kept traveling. The Roman archconfraternity's practices
reached England and North America starting in 1841, carried by the same
currents of devotional revival that moved many Roman customs across the
Atlantic in that period. In France, a dedicated center for the
devotion, the shrine of La Chapelle-Montligeon, was founded in 1884,
giving the custom a pilgrimage home of its own.

By the time the
Catholic Encyclopedia described the Church's "special devotions for
months" in 1911, a plenary indulgence - a full remission of the temporal
punishment owed for sin - for the Holy Souls, obtainable on
any day of November under the usual conditions, was already settled
practice - the whole-month framing was real well before the First World
War, not something invented afterward to fit the moment.

The war did add something, though - not the whole-month devotion, but a
second, narrower one alongside it. In 1915, with the scale of wartime
death weighing on the whole Church, Pope Benedict XV issued the
Apostolic Constitution *Incruentum Altaris Sacrificium*, allowing
priests to offer three Masses on All Souls' Day itself. Around 1917, a
more specific plenary indulgence tied to visiting a cemetery and praying
for the dead on each day from November 1 through 8 is also commonly
credited to him. The two aren't one custom told twice: one is a
general, whole-month grant that predates the war; the other, a later and
narrower grant for cemetery visits, was added during the war - and
both are part of how the month is kept today.

## Why it still matters

None of this is really about where souls go or what happens to them
there - it's about a thousand years of the Church refusing to let the
dead be forgotten. A monk's decision to spend one day a year praying for
everyone who had died grew into a whole month of remembrance, carried
forward by Roman confraternities, an evening bell, a French shrine, and
a wartime pope who widened it further when the need was greatest. That's
still what November asks of anyone who keeps it: visit a grave, light a
candle, say a prayer for someone by name, and keep the living thread
between the Church on earth and everyone who's gone before it.
