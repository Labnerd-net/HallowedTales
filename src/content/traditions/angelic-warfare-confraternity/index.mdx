Long after Thomas Aquinas's own death in 1274, a cord kept at the Dominican house in Vercelli, in the Piedmont region of northern Italy, drew a quiet but steady stream of visitors. According to the story told about Thomas, itself its own legend, from the year his own family held him captive to stop him joining the Dominican order, two angels had once bound a cord around his waist in a vision, promising him lifelong protection of his chastity. Whatever cord the friars at Vercelli kept and showed to pilgrims in the centuries after his death, the devotion built around it was simple and consistent: people came, touched their own cords to the relic, and asked for the same protection it represented.

That informal practice continued for roughly four hundred years before anyone organized it into anything more structured. In the 17th century, a Dominican friar named Franciscus Deurweerders took the devotion that had grown up quietly around Thomas's relic and gave it a formal shape at the Catholic University of Louvain, in what is now Belgium. Members would wear a knotted cord of their own, modeled on the one from the story, and commit together to a shared pursuit of chastity under Thomas's patronage. The devotion spread from there, Dominican house to Dominican house, until in 1727 Pope Benedict XIII granted it formal recognition for the entire Church, elevating what had begun as one university's local practice into something any Catholic anywhere could join. It became known, with a certain amount of period flourish, as the "Militia Angelica," the Angelic Militia, or Angelic Warfare, an echo of the same "angelic" language already attached to Thomas as a thinker, the "Doctor Angelicus," though no source ties the two titles to one documented common origin.

The confraternity is one of three historic devotional associations connected to the Dominican order, and unlike a great many once-popular Catholic customs, it never fully disappeared. It remains active today as an international association of men and women who commit to the pursuit of chastity, usually marked by wearing a small knotted cord beneath their clothing or a medal depicting, on one side, the scene of Thomas being girded by the angels, and on the other, Our Lady of the Rosary. New members are formally enrolled by a priest, in a brief ceremony that still echoes the original devotion at Vercelli: a cord, blessed and given, standing in for the one in the story.

The scene behind all of it became a recognizable subject in Catholic art remarkably early, which is itself a kind of evidence for how quickly the devotion took hold. A small gothic panel painting by the Florentine artist Bernardo Daddi survives from 1338, painted, notably, within about fifteen years of Thomas's 1323 canonization, meaning the story was already settled enough to appear in devotional art almost as soon as the Church had formally recognized him as a saint at all. Daddi's panel shows the moment plainly: Thomas kneeling, an angel binding the cord around him, with a cross marked on the chamber door behind them, a quiet visual shorthand for the temptation that had just been overcome. It is a modest, intimate little painting, originally made as part of a larger altarpiece, and it captures the devotion at roughly the same scale it was still being practiced at: not a grand public spectacle, but something closer to a private, personal commitment, made cord by cord, one person at a time, for nearly seven hundred years since.
