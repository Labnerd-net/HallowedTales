Epiphany, the feast on January 6th marking the Magi's visit to the infant
Christ, is also called Twelfth Night, the twelfth day after Christmas. In
much of the Catholic world it's marked with a ring-shaped cake with a
small prize baked or tucked inside. Whoever finds it in their slice gets
a role to play, a crown to wear, or a duty to take on, depending on where
the tradition is practiced.

The custom's hidden token started out as a single dried bean, the
*fève*, baked directly into the dough. Bakers eventually swapped it for a
small porcelain charm, since a diner could otherwise just feel around
for the lump and dodge it. The French word stuck to the charm even after
it stopped being a bean at all, and "fève" is still the word collectors
use for the tiny ceramic figures some French bakeries now issue by theme
every year.

France itself splits the custom in two. Northern France bakes a
*galette des rois*, a round of puff pastry filled with almond
frangipane; the south favors a crown-shaped brioche called a
*gâteau des rois*, studded with candied fruit. Either way, the cake
comes with a paper crown, and whoever bites into the fève is named king
or queen for the day.

In Spain and across Mexico and Central America, the Epiphany bread is a
*rosca de reyes*, a wreath of sweet brioche decorated with candied
fruit meant to look like jewels in a crown. The hidden token is a tiny
figurine of the baby Jesus, recalling the Holy Family's flight into
Egypt. Finding it carries an obligation rather than just an honor: in
many families, whoever gets the figurine is expected to host a party, or
bring tamales to one, on Candlemas, February 2nd, a full month after the
cake itself was cut.

Louisiana's version grew out of the same French Catholic custom, carried
over by early settlers and stretched to cover the whole Carnival season,
from Epiphany to Fat Tuesday. The New Orleans king cake is a braided ring
of cinnamon-swirled brioche, iced in white and topped with sugar in
purple, green, and gold, the colors of Mardi Gras. A small plastic baby,
standing in for the fève, is tucked into the cake after baking, and
finding it traditionally means you're on the hook to supply the next
king cake, or host the next party, before Lent begins.

## Why it still matters

Three very different customs - a crown-shaped bread, a candied-fruit
wreath, a cinnamon ring under Mardi Gras sugar - are all still doing the
same job: turning a single slice of cake into a small, yearly game of
chance tied to a specific feast. In Louisiana especially, the custom has
outgrown its religious calendar entirely - bakeries sell king cakes
nonstop from Epiphany through Fat Tuesday - but the shape of the game
hasn't changed since a French settler in 1649 Acadia owed his landlord a
cake with a bean baked into the edge.
