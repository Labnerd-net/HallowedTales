In the middle of the Second World War, Pope Pius XII did something popes
don't do lightly: on October 31, 1942, he consecrated the entire world to
the Immaculate Heart of Mary - the Blessed Virgin's heart, honored as a
symbol of her total love for God and her compassion for humanity. He was
acting on reports out of Fatima, Portugal, where three shepherd children
said Mary had appeared to them in 1917 and asked for exactly this kind of
devotion to her heart. One of those children, Lucia dos Santos, grew up
to describe further visions in the mid-1920s that gave the request more
shape. Two years after the world consecration, on August 22, 1944, Pius
XII formally instituted the Feast of the Immaculate Heart of Mary for the
whole Church, choosing the octave day of the Assumption and invoking it
explicitly as a plea for peace. That 1944 decree is the reason August
belongs to this devotion at all - not an ancient custom, but a
20th-century date fixed in wartime.

The devotion itself, though, is much older than Fatima, and it has its
own separate story. In the 13th century, mystics St. Mechthild and St.
Gertrude wrote about Mary's heart as an object of prayerful meditation,
and St. Bernardine of Siena later carried the theme further - but these
were personal spiritual writings, not an instituted feast. The actual
founding moment came centuries later: in 1648, a priest named St. John
Eudes, with his bishop's permission, celebrated the first Feast of the
Holy Heart of Mary at the cathedral of Autun, France, writing his own
Mass and Office for the occasion. Eudes is a figure worth knowing on his
own terms - he's remembered today for founding a devotion to Mary's heart
decades before he turned to a parallel devotion to the Sacred Heart of
Jesus, which he first celebrated in 1672. That ordering surprises people
who assume the two devotions arrived together: Eudes's Marian feast came
first, and it predates the Sacred Heart visions reported by St. Margaret
Mary Alacoque in 1673-75 by a full generation.

For more than a century after Autun, the feast stayed exactly that kind
of patchwork - a local permission here, a diocesan grant there. Palermo
received its own license to celebrate a feast of the "Most Pure Heart of
Mary" in 1799, one of several such grants across the 18th and 19th
centuries, but none of them added up to a universal observance. It took
Fatima, and Pius XII's wartime consecration, to finally give the
devotion the single fixed date - and the single month - it has today.

## Why it still matters

August's claim on the Immaculate Heart of Mary isn't the seamless,
centuries-deep tradition it's sometimes made out to be - it's two real
devotions, separated by nearly three hundred years, that happen to share
a name. St. John Eudes's 1648 feast and Pius XII's 1944 feast were each,
in their own moment, a deliberate choice to turn toward Mary's heart as a
model of love and compassion. Knowing both halves of the story, rather
than smoothing them into one, makes the 1944 date more interesting, not
less: a pope reaching for a 300-year-old devotional thread in the middle
of a world war, and pulling it taut enough to last.
