Each February 3rd, many parishes hold a brief rite where a priest holds two
candles crossed in an X shape against a person's throat, or sometimes just
below the chin, and prays for protection from illnesses of the throat.

The custom traces back to a legend about Blaise, a bishop and physician in
Sebaste, in present-day Turkey. According to the story, a mother brought
her young son to him, choking on a fishbone and near death. Blaise prayed
over the boy, and the bone came loose. The boy lived - and Blaise became,
from then on, the saint people turned to for throat trouble.

The crossed candles themselves come from a separate detail in his
legendary biography: before his martyrdom, Blaise is said to have blessed a
candle brought to him in his prison cell. Over time, the two details fused
into the single rite still performed today.

## Why it still matters

The throat blessing survives almost entirely in parishes that have kept it
as a once-a-year tradition, often with a line of people waiting after Mass.
It's a good example of how a single specific, almost small-scale legend - 
one boy, one fishbone - can anchor a ritual that's still meaningful to
people who've never heard the story behind it.
