The tilma hangs today behind glass above the main altar of the Basilica
of Our Lady of Guadalupe, a short walk from the base of Tepeyac Hill in
Mexico City, where the story holds that it first appeared in December of
1531, carried down from the hill by Juan Diego, an Indigenous convert in
early colonial Mexico. It is a plain, coarse cloth, woven from maguey
fiber - the same material, and the same weave, that any farmer or
laborer of his generation would have worn as an everyday cloak. By every
ordinary
expectation, a cloth like this should not have lasted this long.
Textile conservators generally reckon that unprotected maguey fiber
breaks down within a few decades, and for most of its history the tilma
hung exposed to open air, humidity, and the smoke of thousands of votive
candles, without any varnish or protective coating. It has been
venerated, by that same reckoning, for nearly five hundred years.

The image on the cloth has drawn formal study more than once - infrared
photography, close examination of the pigments, close looks at the
brushwork, if brushwork is what it is. The findings have not settled
into agreement. Some examiners have reported finding no underdrawing or
sizing beneath the image at all, a kind of preparation nearly every
painted work of the period would be expected to have. Others have
reported the opposite: visible sketch lines in the folds of the robe, a
seam where the hands appear to have been retouched, and a limited,
ordinary palette of period pigments - pine soot for black, local earth
tones, carmine red, and gold. The two readings do not agree, and this is
not the place to settle which is right. Readers who want the detailed
evidentiary record can find The Miracle Register's own published
coverage of the same examinations.

## A relic that survived a bomb

What the tilma's long public history does offer, without needing any
laboratory to confirm it, is a single well-documented afternoon in 1921.
On November 14 of that year, a government employee named Luciano Pérez
Carpio slipped into the Basilica with a bouquet of flowers, a bomb
hidden inside it, and set it at the foot of the altar, directly beneath
the tilma. The explosion, heard a kilometer away, bent the basilica's
bronze candelabra into twisted metal, snapped the altar crucifix, and
cracked the marble steps beneath it. When the smoke cleared, the tilma
itself, and even the glass case built to protect it, were untouched -
not so much as a hairline crack in the pane. The bent crucifix still
stands on display at the Basilica today, kept as it was left, a plain
physical reminder of that morning rather than a retelling of it.

Whatever side of the scientific question a visitor comes down on, the
pilgrimage itself has never paused for the debate. Millions come to
stand before the tilma every year, making the Basilica of Our Lady of
Guadalupe the most visited Marian shrine anywhere in the world. The
crowds come heaviest each December 12, when the feast draws people who
have walked, or in many cases crossed the final stretch of the plaza on
their knees, to stand for a moment in front of the same cloth that has
hung there, by the account handed down, since Juan Diego first let it
fall open before a bishop who had asked for nothing more than a sign.
