As Christ carried his cross through the streets toward Calvary, tradition
holds that a woman stepped forward from the crowd and offered him her
veil to wipe the sweat and blood from his face. When he handed it back,
his image remained, pressed into the cloth without brush or pigment - a
portrait made, according to the legend, by contact alone.

The scene doesn't appear anywhere in the Gospels, and it takes thirteen
centuries after the Crucifixion to find it written down at all. Around
1160, Pietro Mallius, a canon of Saint Peter's Basilica, proposed the story
as an explanation for a relic Rome already possessed and already called
Veronica's veil. Two pilgrims visiting Rome in 1199 left the earliest
firsthand accounts of actually seeing it.

## A name with its own history

The woman's name has a stranger backstory than the veil itself. Centuries
before anyone wove the wiping-of-the-face story, apocryphal writers from
the 4th and 5th centuries had already attached the name to a different
figure entirely: the woman healed of a hemorrhage by touching Christ's
cloak in Luke's Gospel, whom these texts called Berenike, Latinized as
Veronica. How that name migrated onto an entirely separate relic and
legend isn't fully clear - one long-standing theory sidesteps the
question altogether, suggesting "Veronica" began as a corruption of the
Latin phrase *vera icon*, "true image," and only later hardened into a
woman's name.

The veil belongs to a small, specific category of Christian image called
*acheiropoieta* - "not made by hands" - images believed to have formed
without any human artist, a tradition it shares with other legendary
images venerated across the Church. Rome's veil has rarely been shown to
the public in modern times, which if anything has only deepened the
legend around exactly what the keepers of Saint Peter's are protecting.
