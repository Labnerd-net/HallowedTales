John's Gospel notes an odd detail about Christ's clothing at the
Crucifixion: the soldiers divided up his garments but left his tunic
whole, since it had been "woven in one piece," and gambled for it instead
of tearing it. That single seamless robe became its own relic tradition -
and, as with the Holy Lance, one claimed by two different places at once.

## Two robes, two emperors

Trier Cathedral in Germany credits its tunic to Saint Helena, mother of the
emperor Constantine, who tradition says sent it from Jerusalem around
327-328 after her own pilgrimage there. It's a tidy story, but the
document meant to prove it - a charter supposedly issued by Pope
Sylvester - is widely considered a later forgery rather than an authentic
4th-century record. What can actually be documented is an altar
consecration in Trier in 1196 that is recorded as containing the robe,
with later accounts claiming, without evidence, that it had already been
displayed there before.

Argenteuil's tunic, kept today at the Basilique Saint-Denys outside
Paris, traces its own origin to Charlemagne, who tradition holds received
the garment as a coronation gift from the Byzantine Empress Irene around
800 and entrusted it to his daughter Theodrade, abbess of Argenteuil's
monastery. Its earliest supporting document dates to 1156 - forty years
earlier than Trier's, though both still land squarely in the medieval
period rather than the age of Charlemagne or Helena.

Neither city has ever seriously pressed its claim against the other;
exposition of either robe is rare enough that each draws enormous crowds
on its own terms whenever it happens. Trier's most recent exposition drew
widespread attention in 2026, when Pope Leo XIV became the first pope to
venerate the tunic there in person - a reminder that, rare claimants or
not, the devotion around this relic is very much a living one.
