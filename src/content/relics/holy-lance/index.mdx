John's Gospel records that after Christ died on the cross, a soldier
pierced his side to confirm it. Later tradition gave that soldier a name,
Longinus, and turned his spear into one of the most sought-after relics in
Christendom. The trail is quiet for the first several centuries, but by
around 570 a pilgrim named Antoninus of Piacenza was describing a lance
kept among the Passion relics venerated on Jerusalem's Mount Zion -
alongside, intriguingly, the Crown of Thorns.

From Jerusalem the story splits, and splits again, until three different
treasuries each hold what they call the Holy Lance.

## Three lances, one wound

The Vatican's lance, kept beneath the dome of Saint Peter's Basilica, traces
its documented history furthest back toward that 6th-century Jerusalem
account. It reached Rome by an unusually worldly route: in 1492, the
Ottoman Sultan Bayezid II sent it to Pope Innocent VIII as a diplomatic
gift, part of a bargain to keep the Sultan's exiled brother and rival
claimant safely out of the way in papal custody.

Vienna's lance tells a different story. Bound in a gold sleeve for
centuries, it traveled as part of the Imperial Regalia of the Holy Roman
Empire, carried out for coronation after coronation. A medieval
inscription once identified a nail fused into the blade as one of the
nails of the Crucifixion, layering one relic tradition on top of another.
Modern metallurgical study dates the blade itself to roughly the 7th or
8th century - not a 1st-century Roman weapon, but a genuinely old and
genuinely venerated one.

The third claimant, the Geghard held at Etchmiadzin in Armenia, comes with
its own apostolic origin story: tradition says the Apostle Thaddeus
brought it to Armenia himself in the year 33. The earliest text that
actually names it, though, is a 13th-century Armenian manuscript - a gap
of well over a thousand years between the claimed event and the first
record of it.

None of the three claims can be proven, and none of the three treasuries
seems especially bothered by that. As with the Crown of Thorns and the
Holy Tunic, the Holy Lance's real story may be less about which blade
touched Christ's side and more about how many different communities
needed, across the centuries, to feel close to that moment.
