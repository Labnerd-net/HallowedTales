In the early 4th century, Helena - mother of the emperor Constantine and
already elderly by the standards of her time - traveled to Jerusalem to
find the relics of Christ's Passion. According to the legend that grew up
around her journey, she located the site of the crucifixion and had it
excavated, uncovering three crosses buried beneath it. To determine which
one was Christ's, she had a gravely ill woman - or, in some versions, a
corpse being carried past - brought to touch each cross in turn. Two did
nothing. The third restored the woman to health, or raised the dead man,
and so the True Cross was identified.

Helena is said to have divided the relic, leaving a portion in Jerusalem
and carrying the rest back to Rome, where it rests today at the basilica of
Santa Croce in Gerusalemme - built, according to tradition, specifically to
house it.

## A relic that multiplied

What makes the True Cross different from most relics on this site is scale.
Small fragments were distributed, gifted, and in some cases simply claimed
by churches across medieval Europe for centuries, until pieces of "the True
Cross" could be found in hundreds of locations at once. Rather than
undermine the tradition, this became part of its own folklore - a relic so
holy that it could be divided endlessly without ever running out, each
fragment carrying the full weight of the whole.

The Feast of the Exaltation of the Holy Cross, celebrated each September
14th, traces directly back to this discovery story, commemorating both
Helena's find and the later recovery of the relic after it was carried off
during a 7th-century war.
