The Gospels describe Roman soldiers twisting thorn branches into a crown
and pressing it onto Christ's head before the crucifixion, mocking him as
"King of the Jews." Within a few generations, Christians in Jerusalem
were venerating what they believed to be that same crown - the writer
Paulinus of Nola mentions thorn relics there in the early 5th century, and
by 591 the historian Gregory of Tours was describing a crown that
supposedly stayed green with living leaves, year after year.

## A relic with a paper trail

Unlike most objects in this pillar, the Crown of Thorns comes with a
documented chain of custody that reads less like legend and more like
diplomatic history. By 1063 it had been moved to Constantinople, where a
reliquary crafted around 950 already lists a fragment of it among the
Byzantine emperors' treasures. When the Latin Emperor Baldwin II found
himself short on funds in the 1230s, he pawned the crown to Venetian
merchants as collateral for a loan - and it was King Louis IX of France
who redeemed it, in 1238.

Louis didn't simply store the relic; he built an entire chapel for it.
The Sainte-Chapelle, completed in 1248 on the grounds of the royal palace
in Paris, was designed as a jeweled reliquary in architectural form, and
on August 19, 1239, Louis carried the crown into the city himself in a
procession commemorated ever since. It stayed there until the French
Revolution sent it to the Bibliothèque Nationale for safekeeping; the 1801
Concordat between France and the Vatican returned it to the Church, this
time to Notre-Dame de Paris, where it remains.

That long survival nearly ended on April 15, 2019, when fire tore through
Notre-Dame's roof and spire. Firefighters and a cathedral chaplain
carried the reliquary to safety that night, and it returned to public
veneration when the restored cathedral reopened - a crown that has now
outlasted a pawnshop, a revolution, and a fire.
