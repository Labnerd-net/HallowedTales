Matthew's Gospel gives the Magi no names, no fixed number, and no ending
- they appear bearing gifts for the infant Christ and then simply "depart
into their own country," never mentioned again. Later tradition filled
in everything the Gospel left out: three kings, named Melchior, Gaspar,
and Balthazar, representing the known continents of the ancient world,
and - eventually - a set of bones said to be theirs.

Tradition credits Eustorgius I, bishop of Milan, with bringing those
relics from Constantinople to his city in 344, and for the next eight
centuries the Basilica of Sant'Eustorgio in Milan became a major
pilgrimage stop on the strength of that claim alone.

## From conquest to cathedral

The relics' next move had nothing to do with devotion. In 1162, the Holy
Roman Emperor Frederick Barbarossa took Milan by force, and his forces
carried off the bones of the Magi along with the rest of the city's
plunder. Barbarossa handed them to his imperial chancellor for Italy,
Rainald von Dassel, who was, conveniently, also the Archbishop of
Cologne, and in 1164 the relics arrived in that city.

Cologne treated them as a genuine treasure from the start. Between 1180 and 1225,
the master goldsmith Nicholas of Verdun built the relics a shrine shaped
like a miniature basilica in solid gold, still the largest reliquary of
its kind from the medieval period. The crowds the shrine drew were large
enough that they're part of the reason Cologne Cathedral itself - one of
the largest Gothic cathedrals ever built - broke ground in 1248: the
existing church simply couldn't hold the pilgrims anymore.

Milan never fully let go of its original claim. In 1903 and 1904, small
fragments of bone and garment were returned from Cologne to
Sant'Eustorgio, where they rest today beside the empty sarcophagus the
relics left behind nearly 750 years earlier - a quiet coda to a story
that began with gifts for a newborn king and passed, along the way,
through the hands of an emperor at war.
