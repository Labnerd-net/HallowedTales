The familiar version of this story, Helena excavating three buried crosses and identifying the true one by touching it to a dying woman, is really a harmonized version, smoothed down from several ancient accounts that do not actually agree with each other. Read the sources in the order they were written, and the legend visibly grows with each retelling.

## Three tellings, one lifetime apart

The earliest surviving full account comes from Ambrose of Milan, in a funeral oration he gave for the emperor Theodosius in 395. In Ambrose's version, Helena finds the three crosses but identifies Christ's by reading the titulus, the inscribed plaque reading "Jesus of Nazareth, King of the Jews," still fixed to it. There is no dying woman, no miracle of healing at all. Instead, Ambrose has Helena order two of the crucifixion nails made into relics of a different kind: one worked into a bridle bit, one into a jeweled diadem, both sent on to Constantine, who is said to have used them and passed them down to the emperors who followed him.

A different version, compiled around 402 by Rufinus of Aquileia as a continuation to his Latin edition of Eusebius's Church History, drops the titulus entirely and introduces the scene most people now know: a pagan temple to Aphrodite, built directly over the site, torn down on Helena's orders; three crosses found in the rubble; and Bishop Macarius of Jerusalem settling which was Christ's by bringing a mortally ill woman to touch each one in turn, with only the third restoring her. Rufinus's account draws on an earlier Greek history, now lost, written by Cyril of Jerusalem's own nephew Gelasius of Caesarea before 395, which suggests this version of the story was already circulating in the East before Ambrose gave his oration in the West.

By the time the fifth-century church historians Socrates Scholasticus, Sozomen, and Theodoret wrote their own versions, the two earlier tellings had simply been fused: the temple, the three crosses, Macarius, the dying woman, and the titulus all appear together in the same scene, as if the story had always been told that way. It hadn't. It took roughly two generations for Christian writers to agree on how the most famous relic-discovery story in the Church had actually happened.

## Older than the story itself

Stranger still, the relic's fame predates any of these accounts. Cyril of Jerusalem, writing his Catechetical Lectures in 348, well before Ambrose or Rufinus or anyone else set down how the cross was found, simply states that its wood "is still, until this day, visible at our place" and had "now almost filled the whole world" through pieces taken from it by the faithful. Cyril does not tell a discovery story at all. He writes as though the relic's presence in Jerusalem, and its fragments' spread across the Christian world, were already an unremarkable fact of life a generation after Helena's supposed journey, decades before anyone thought to write down how it had gotten there.

## From Helena to Heraclius

The medieval period went further still, stitching Helena's story into something much larger. The 13th-century collection of saints' lives known as the Golden Legend traces the cross's wood back to a tree said to have grown from a seed placed in Adam's mouth at his burial, carries it forward through Solomon's Temple and the Queen of Sheba, and only then arrives at Helena's discovery, before continuing on to the Byzantine emperor Heraclius, who recovered the relic in 628 after the Persians had carried it off as war plunder decades earlier. That later recovery, not the original discovery, is what the Feast of the Exaltation of the Holy Cross on September 14 actually commemorates.

It was this full, centuries-spanning version of the legend, Adam to Helena to Heraclius, that the Renaissance painter Piero della Francesca set on the walls of the Basilica of San Francesco in Arezzo between 1452 and 1466, one of the great fresco cycles of the period. Helena's excavation is only one scene among many there, flanked by battles, visions, and the slow passage of a single piece of wood through centuries of human history, each generation adding its own chapter to a story that, as the earliest sources themselves show, was never told quite the same way twice.
