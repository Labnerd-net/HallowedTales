Tradition holds that these twenty-eight marble steps are the very stairs
Christ climbed to face trial before Pontius Pilate in Jerusalem, and that
St. Helena - already credited with recovering the True Cross and sending
holy relics to Trier - had them carried to Rome around the year 326.
Nothing in the written record actually backs up that date; the earliest
clear evidence of pilgrims treating the stairs as sacred comes over a
thousand years later, at the Jubilee of 1450.

By then, the devotion itself was unmistakable, and it has never really
stopped: pilgrims climb the stairs on their knees, one at a time, pausing
to pray on each step in memory of Christ's Passion. The German monk
Martin Luther is recorded making the climb in 1510, reciting the Our
Father at every step - decades before he would break from Rome entirely.

## A staircase that outlived its building

The stairs themselves have survived longer than the structure they
originally stood in. When Pope Sixtus V had the ruined medieval Lateran
Palace torn down in 1589, he had the stairs carefully relocated rather
than left to the wreckage, commissioning the architect Domenico Fontana
to build them a new home facing the Sancta Sanctorum, the popes' own
private chapel - still their location today.

Centuries of knees wore grooves into the marble, so in 1724 Pope Benedict
XIII had the steps covered in protective wood, leaving pilgrims to climb
atop boards rather than the original stone. That changed only briefly,
in 2019, when the stairs were uncovered for several months and pilgrims
could once again kneel on the same marble worn smooth since the Middle
Ages - a small window onto centuries of exactly the same devotion still
drawing people to climb.
