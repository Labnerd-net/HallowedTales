This story comes down through The Golden Legend, and it begins with George - a soldier and martyr
of the early Church who later became the patron saint of England - riding into trouble that had
already cost the town of Silene most of its sheep.

A dragon had settled near the town, poisoning the countryside with its breath, and demanded a
steady tribute of sheep to stay away. When the sheep ran out, the town turned to drawing lots
among its own children instead, and the lot eventually fell on the king's daughter.

George found her waiting for the dragon, already resigned to what was coming. He challenged it
anyway - wounded it with his lance, then had the princess loop her girdle around its neck so it
followed her back toward the city like a leashed dog, entirely subdued. Once there, he put the
terms to the townspeople plainly: he'd finish the dragon off for good, if they agreed to be
baptized. They agreed. By some tellings, thousands of them were baptized that same day.

## A legend grafted onto an older saint

<InlineImage
  src={vatopediGeorgeIcon}
  alt="Byzantine ivory relief icon of Saint George standing in military dress holding a spear and shield, with no dragon present"
  caption="Saint George as standing warrior-martyr, 11th-century ivory relief, Vatopedi Monastery, Mount Athos"
  credit="Wikimedia Commons, public domain"
  side="left"
/>

George was venerated as a martyr for nearly a thousand years before any surviving text mentions a
dragon at all. The devotion runs back to at least the fifth century in the Eastern Church, built
entirely on his death under persecution, with no monster anywhere in it. The dragon arrived later,
likely carried west by crusaders who'd picked up some version of the story in the East, and it was
The Golden Legend, in the thirteenth century, that locked it into the form still told today.
