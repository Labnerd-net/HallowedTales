Gubbio had been terrorized by a wolf for a long time, according to the story told about Francis
of Assisi - the 13th-century friar who founded the Franciscans. It started with sheep and dogs,
then, as the wolf grew bolder, moved to the townspeople themselves, until no one felt safe leaving
without a weapon.

That was the situation Francis found when he arrived. He decided, against everyone's advice, to
go out and find the wolf himself.

He walked out through the town gate alone, after his companions refused to go any farther. When
the wolf spotted him, it charged with its jaws open - and Francis made the sign of the cross and
ordered it, in Christ's name, to stop.

That was enough. The wolf's charge broke off, and it came the rest of the way to Francis and lay
down at his feet like a tamed animal.

What followed was a kind of bargain. Francis told the wolf plainly that it had done wrong - it had
killed creatures that belonged to God, including people - but offered it a way out: if it gave up
killing, the town would feed it for the rest of its life. The wolf bowed its head and placed a paw
in Francis's open hand, as if sealing the deal.

Francis brought the wolf back into Gubbio with him. The whole town gathered in the marketplace to
see it, and Francis preached on repentance before asking the townspeople to hold up their end of
the bargain. They agreed, and the wolf placed its paw in his hand once more, in front of everyone.

By this account, the wolf lived peacefully among the townspeople for two years afterward, going
door to door to be fed and harming no one - so tame that even the local dogs stopped barking at
it. When it eventually died of old age, the town mourned it as they would one of their own.

## A beloved story, added later

Few stories about Francis are as loved as this one, and few arrived this late. It surfaces for
the first time in the Fioretti, the fourteenth-century collection known as the Little Flowers of
Saint Francis, compiled roughly a century after his death - and neither of his two earliest
biographers, Thomas of Celano or Bonaventure, mentions a wolf at all. That absence doesn't make
the story any less loved; if anything, it shows how a saint already known for talking to birds
became, over a few generations of retelling, exactly the kind of figure a frightened town would
imagine striking a deal with.
