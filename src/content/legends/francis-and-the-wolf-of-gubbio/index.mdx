The town of Gubbio, the story goes, had been terrorized for some time by a
wolf so large and vicious that it had taken to killing not just livestock
but people who crossed its path, until no one dared leave the town walls
unarmed. When Francis of Assisi - the 13th-century friar who founded the
Franciscans and is remembered for his love of animals and the poor -
arrived and heard of it, he decided to go out and meet the wolf himself,
over the frightened objections of the townspeople.

He found the wolf in the woods, and when it charged him, Francis made the
sign of the cross and addressed it as "Brother Wolf," ordering it in
Christ's name to stop its attacks. The wolf, by every account, calmed
immediately and lay down at his feet. Francis then negotiated what
amounted to a peace treaty: the townspeople would feed the wolf for the
rest of its life, and in exchange, it would never harm another person or
animal in Gubbio again. The wolf lived out its days wandering peacefully
from door to door, fed by the same people it had once hunted, until it
died of old age. At that point, the story says, the whole town mourned
it.

## A beloved story, added later

Few stories about Francis are as beloved as this one, and few are as late
an addition. It appears for the first time in the Fioretti, the
14th-century collection known as the Little Flowers of Saint Francis,
compiled around a century after his death, and neither Thomas of Celano
nor Bonaventure, the Franciscan theologian - his two earliest biographers
- mentions a wolf at all. That absence doesn't make it any less beloved;
if anything, it shows how a saint already known for talking to birds
became, over a few generations of storytelling, exactly the kind of
figure a frightened town would imagine negotiating with a wolf.
