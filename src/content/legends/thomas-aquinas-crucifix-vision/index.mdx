By 1273, Thomas Aquinas, a Dominican friar then in his late forties, had spent the better part of three decades becoming one of the most celebrated theologians in Christian Europe, twice holding the most prestigious teaching chair at the University of Paris and filling shelf after shelf with commentary, disputation, and his still-unfinished *Summa Theologiae*. That year found him back in Naples, the city near where he had first studied as a young man, still writing, at that particular moment, on the subject of the Eucharist.

One day, according to the story that has been told about him ever since, Thomas lingered in the chapel of Saint Nicholas at the Dominican convent in Naples before a large crucifix, the kind every chapel of the period would have had above or near its altar. A fellow friar named Dominic of Caserta, serving as the chapel's sacristan, happened to be nearby and later said he witnessed what followed: the figure of the crucified Christ seemed to speak to Thomas directly, saying, "You have written well of me, Thomas. What reward would you have for your labor?"

Thomas's reply is the part of the story that has endured the longest, rendered in Latin as "Domine, non nisi Te," a phrase popular retellings today usually translate as the terse, almost modern-sounding "Nothing but you, Lord." It is a striking line, and it is not hard to see why it has outlasted seven centuries of retelling. But the earliest version of the story that survives in writing, compiled by the French Dominican Bernard of Gui in 1318 as part of the material gathered for Thomas's canonization, renders the Latin a little differently, closer to "I would not accept any other compensation, without you yourself." Both say essentially the same thing, asking for nothing except Christ, but the shorter, punchier modern phrasing rests on one particular reading of ambiguous medieval Latin grammar rather than a single fixed original quotation. Bernard of Gui's account itself was written roughly 45 years after the moment it describes, drawing not on any document from Thomas's own lifetime but on testimony gathered from people who had known him, in the years immediately after his death.

The story is often told alongside, or even folded directly into, another episode from later that same year: the morning of December 6, 1273, the feast of Saint Nicholas, when Thomas was celebrating Mass and underwent some kind of experience so overwhelming that he set down his pen afterward and never seriously picked it up again. When his close companion Reginald of Piperno pressed him to keep writing, Thomas is said to have answered that everything he had written now seemed like so much straw next to what had been shown to him. Whether the crucifix's words and the straw declaration describe one single morning or two separate experiences months apart is not entirely settled by the sources; different retellings over the centuries have folded them together or kept them apart, but both episodes share the same shape: Thomas, having devoted his adult life to writing about God in careful, exhaustive argument, is confronted with something that makes the writing itself feel suddenly, radically insufficient.

The crucifix scene in particular became a recognizable subject for later devotional art. The most striking surviving version is a large 1593 altarpiece by the Florentine painter Santi di Tito, commissioned for a family chapel in the Basilica of San Marco in Florence, the same Dominican church, fittingly, where the Dominican friar and painter Fra Angelico had worked a century and a half earlier. Di Tito shows Thomas kneeling in open-mouthed rapture as the crucified Christ leans forward from the cross as if alive, with several other saints gathered as witnesses in the same sacred space. Some popular versions of the story add a further detail: that Thomas was lifted bodily into the air during the vision, in addition to hearing the crucifix speak. Thomas does appear elsewhere in canonization testimony as someone witnesses reported seeing levitate during prayer on other occasions, but none of the earliest named accounts of this particular scene, neither Bernard of Gui's text nor Di Tito's painting, attach a levitation to this specific moment. It is the kind of detail that tends to accumulate around a story already remarkable enough on its own: a friar, already one of the most celebrated minds of his age, being told by the very crucifix he had spent his life writing about that he had written well, and finding, in that moment, that he wanted nothing else in return.
