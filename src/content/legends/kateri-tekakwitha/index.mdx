Kateri Tekakwitha - a Mohawk girl born in 1656, later the first Native American to be declared
a saint - was four years old when smallpox swept through Ossernenon, the Mohawk village near
present-day Auriesville, New York, where she was born. The epidemic took her
father, her mother, and her infant brother. Kateri survived, but the disease left her face
scarred and her eyesight permanently damaged - she spent the rest of her life partially blind,
feeling her way through a world that had marked her plainly as someone who had suffered.

She was raised afterward by an uncle, a Mohawk chief who took her into his household following
her parents' deaths. Ossernenon itself was destroyed not long after in a French military
campaign and rebuilt nearby as Gandaouagué, on the other side of the Mohawk River - the same
community, a new site. It was there, in 1675, that French Jesuit missionaries came to live
among the Mohawk, and Kateri, already drawn to the faith her own mother had practiced before
her, began speaking with them in secret. She was baptized on Easter Sunday, 1676, and took the
name Catherine - Kateri, in Mohawk pronunciation.

Her baptism cost her the life she had known. She refused to work on Sundays, turned down a
marriage her uncle had arranged, and was accused of sorcery by neighbors who did not accept her
new faith. Children threw stones at her. Food was sometimes withheld from her table. In the
autumn of 1677, with the help of a small group of Christian Mohawk neophytes, Kateri fled
Gandaouagué for good. Her destination was Kahnawake, a mission settlement near Montreal -
known to the Jesuits as Saint-François-Xavier du Sault Saint-Louis - where other Native converts
had already gathered under the missionaries' care. It was a journey of roughly two hundred
miles through woods, rivers, and swamps, and it took close to two months to complete.

At Kahnawake, Kateri found the life she had been reaching for. She attended daily Mass, prayed
for long hours before the Blessed Sacrament, cared for the sick and the elderly, and took on
fasting and penances so demanding that her own Jesuit confessor eventually stepped in and made
her scale them back. She also made a private vow of virginity - something without precedent
among Mohawk women at the time - and was known, despite chronic poor health, for a steady,
visible joy that those around her remembered long after she was gone.

She died at Kahnawake on April 17, 1680, at the age of twenty-four. It is what happened in the
minutes after her death that became the heart of her legend. Claude Chauchetière and Pierre
Cholenec, the two Jesuit missionaries present at her deathbed, both later wrote accounts of her
life. Cholenec's version describes what he says he witnessed directly: within about a quarter
of an hour of her death, the scarring that had marked Kateri's face since childhood began to
fade. By the time others gathered to see her, witnesses said, her skin appeared smooth and
unmarked, as though the twenty years of visible suffering had simply been lifted from her.

Neither priest offered an explanation for what they described. They recorded it the way they
recorded the rest of her life: as something they had seen, set down for the record, left for
others to weigh. Their manuscript biographies became the primary sources for everything the
Church would later know about Kateri Tekakwitha, and this account has traveled with her story
ever since, repeated in the petitions that eventually led to her beatification by Pope John
Paul II in 1980 and her canonization by Pope Benedict XVI in 2012.

The devotion that grew up around her has never really stopped. Her tomb still rests beside the
main altar of the Saint Francis Xavier mission church at Kahnawake, where visitors come
year-round to the shrine built up around her life and where her feast is kept every April 17.
And since 1939, when a bishop in North Dakota first gathered missionaries and Native Catholics
to talk about pastoral life on the Northern Plains, an annual meeting named the Tekakwitha
Conference in her honor has carried on the gathering she never lived to see - Native Catholics
still coming together each year the way the converts at Kahnawake once did.

## How this story changed over time

Cholenec's own account places the fading of Kateri's scars at roughly a quarter of an hour
after her death, a gradual transformation rather than an instant one. Later devotional retellings
have sometimes compressed that timeline further, describing the change as immediate - a
detail worth knowing, since it says more about how the story has been retold over the centuries
than about what the two Jesuit witnesses actually recorded.
