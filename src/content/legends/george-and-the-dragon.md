In the Golden Legend's telling, a dragon had taken up residence near the
city of Silene, poisoning the surrounding countryside with its breath and
demanding a steady tribute of sheep to stay away from the town. When the
sheep ran out, the town turned to drawing lots among its own children, and
the lot eventually fell to the king's own daughter.

George happens upon the princess waiting for the dragon, already resigned
to her fate. He challenges the beast directly, wounds it with his lance,
then has the princess loop her girdle around its neck so it follows behind
her like a leashed dog, entirely subdued. George leads the dragon into the
city and offers its people a bargain: he will finish the creature off for
good, if they agree to be baptized. They agree, and by some tellings,
thousands were baptized that same day.

## A legend grafted onto an older saint

George was already a venerated martyr for roughly a thousand years before
any surviving account mentions a dragon at all - early devotion to him
traces back to at least the 5th century in the Eastern Church, built
entirely around his martyrdom under persecution, with no monster in
sight. The dragon episode seems to have entered Western tradition later,
likely carried home by crusaders who had encountered some version of the
story in the East, and it was the Golden Legend, compiled in the 13th
century, that locked the tale into the form still told today - a clear
example of a popular saint accumulating new legendary material centuries
after his own lifetime.
