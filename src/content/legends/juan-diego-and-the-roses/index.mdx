Juan Diego was born Cuauhtlatoatzin - "the eagle who speaks" - around
1474, in the farming village of Cuauhtitlan north of what is now Mexico
City. He belonged to the Chichimeca people and made his living as a
farmer and a weaver of mats. In the early 1520s, a decade after the
Spanish conquest of the Aztec empire, Franciscan missionaries baptized
him and his wife among the first wave of Indigenous converts in the new
colony, and he took the Christian name Juan Diego. Widowed not long
after, he gave himself over to his faith entirely, walking some fifteen
miles each way, several times a week, to attend Mass and receive
instruction in Tlatelolco.

On the morning of December 9, 1531, hurrying along that same road, Juan
Diego passed the base of a hill called Tepeyac. He heard music, like
birdsong, coming from its summit, then a woman's voice calling his name.
Climbing to see who had called him, he found a young woman standing in a
light brighter than the sun, dressed, by every description handed down,
like a noblewoman of his own people rather than a Spaniard. She told him
she was the Virgin Mary, mother of the true God, and asked that a chapel
be built on that spot, where she promised to show her compassion to all
who came to her there. Juan Diego carried the request to Bishop Juan de
Zumárraga, the first bishop of Mexico. The bishop received him politely
and asked for a sign before he would act on a peasant's report of a
vision.

Mary appeared to Juan Diego a second time on December 12, as he hurried
past Tepeyac again, this time on an errand to find a priest for his
uncle Juan Bernardino, whom he believed to be dying. She told him his
uncle had already been healed, and she sent him to the top of the hill,
bare and rocky and far past any growing season, to gather flowers as the
sign the bishop had asked for. He found Castilian roses blooming there in
the December cold. He gathered them into his tilma - the coarse cloak,
woven from maguey fiber, worn by men of his class - and carried them,
cradled against his chest, back to the bishop's house. When he opened
the cloak to let the roses fall at Zumárraga's feet, the bishop and his
household saw that an image of the Virgin, exactly as Juan Diego had
described her, had appeared on the cloth itself.

A chapel rose at Tepeyac within the year, the first of what would grow
into the Basilica of Our Lady of Guadalupe, still the most visited
Marian shrine in the world. Juan Diego spent his remaining years in a
small hut beside it, caring for pilgrims, until his death in 1548.

## How much of this can be verified

The story as it is told today comes down through the Nican Mopohua -
"Thus It Is Told" - a Nahuatl-language account that tradition credits to
Antonio Valeriano, an Indigenous scholar educated by the same
Franciscans, writing perhaps twenty-five years after the events it
describes. No manuscript from Valeriano's own lifetime has ever
surfaced. The earliest copy known to survive is a printing from 1649,
nearly 120 years after the apparitions, published by a priest named Luis
Lasso de la Vega. That gap has drawn serious scrutiny. Bishop Zumárraga,
who stands at the center of the story, left behind a substantial body of
his own correspondence, and none of it mentions Juan Diego or any
apparition at Tepeyac. The historian Joaquín García Icazbalceta raised
the problem privately as early as 1883. Asked to help build the case for
Juan Diego's sainthood, he concluded instead that he could find no
contemporary record to support it.

The question did not stay buried. It resurfaced publicly in 2002, when
Guillermo Schulenburg, a former abbot of the Basilica of Guadalupe
itself, wrote to the Vatican's Secretary of State raising the same
concern on the eve of the canonization.

The Church canonized Juan Diego that year regardless, on July 31, 2002,
naming him the first Indigenous saint of the Americas canonized in the
modern era - and the historical question of just how much can be
verified about the man behind the story has never been resolved in
either direction since. What has endured without any such dispute is the
devotion itself: every December 12, families across Mexico and the
Mexican diaspora mark the feast with predawn serenades to the Virgin,
pilgrims approaching the Basilica on their knees across the plaza, and
home altars built around whatever image of Our Lady of Guadalupe a
household happens to keep - the same image, the story holds, that first
appeared in a bundle of roses, on a hillside north of the city, nearly
five hundred years ago.
