Of everything that happened during the year Thomas Aquinas's own family held him captive to stop him from becoming a Dominican friar, one episode has outlasted all the rest in the telling: the night, as the story goes, that his family sent a woman into his room hoping to break him in the one way reasoned argument could not.

The year was around 1244. Thomas, then nineteen, had announced his intention to join the Dominicans, a young and still somewhat controversial order of mendicant friars who owned nothing and begged for their food, a step down in status, as his noble family saw it, from the comfortable Benedictine abbacy they had always assumed would be his. Unable to talk him out of it, his brothers had him intercepted on the road and held at the family's own castles, hoping that enough time and pressure would change his mind. When persuasion failed, the story goes that his brothers tried something more direct: they sent a woman into the room where Thomas was being kept, intending to tempt him into breaking the vow of chastity he had already taken. Thomas's response, as it has been told ever since, was immediate and physical: he grabbed a burning brand from the fireplace and drove her from the room, and by some versions of the story marked the door behind her with the sign of the cross.

Thomas reportedly kept what followed to himself for the rest of his life. Only once, late in his career, did he confide the rest of it to Reginald of Piperno, the Dominican friar who had become his closest companion and confessor, which is itself worth noting, since it means the entire rest of the story rests on a single named witness, disclosed decades after the fact. According to what Thomas told Reginald, he fell into a kind of mystical sleep after driving the woman away, and in it two angels appeared to him and bound a cord around his waist, telling him that he was being girded, on God's own authority, with a girdle of chastity that would never afterward be at risk. Thomas is said to have worn that cord, hidden beneath his habit, for the rest of his life without ever speaking of it again until that one late conversation with Reginald.

The physical object associated with the story did not disappear with Thomas's death in 1274. According to devotional tradition, it was given that same year to the Dominican house at Vercelli, in the Piedmont region of northern Italy, the mother-house of Thomas's own province, by John of Vercelli, who had just become the Dominican order's Master General. Pilgrims came to touch their own cords to the relic, asking for the same protection it represented, a practice that continued for centuries. Devotional sources hold that the relic was later moved to the town of Chieri, near Turin, where a version of it is still venerated today, though, as with many relics of this kind, its authenticity as the literal cord from the story is a matter of unbroken devotional tradition rather than something that can be independently verified.

The scene became a recognizable one in Catholic devotional art surprisingly early. A small panel painting by the Florentine artist Bernardo Daddi, showing the temptress fleeing as an angel binds the cord around Thomas, survives from 1338, painted within about fifteen years of Thomas's 1323 canonization, suggesting the story was already a settled part of how he was remembered almost immediately after the Church recognized him as a saint. Nearly three centuries later, the Spanish painter Diego Velazquez took up the same subject in a large, dramatic canvas completed in 1632: Thomas kneeling, held upright by one angel while a second binds the cord around his waist, a small figure still visible through a doorway in the background. For a long stretch of its history, that painting, now in the Museum of Sacred Art at Orihuela Cathedral in southern Spain, was credited to a lesser-known local painter, and it was only in the 1920s that art historians recognized it as an authentic, early work by Velazquez himself.

The episode did not stay confined to paintings and private devotion. Centuries after Thomas's death, Dominican friars in Belgium formalized the practice of praying for chastity while wearing a cord modeled on the one from the story, eventually organizing it into a confraternity that still exists today, a living custom with its own, separate account of how the legend behind it became a devotion Catholics actually practice.
