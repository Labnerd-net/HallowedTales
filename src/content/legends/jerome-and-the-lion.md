One afternoon, as the story is told, a lion, holding up one paw, limped
into the monastery courtyard near Bethlehem where Jerome - the
4th-century scholar who translated the Bible into Latin - lived and
worked, and the other monks scattered in terror. Jerome alone stayed put, examined the paw, and
drew out a long thorn lodged deep inside it. Grateful, the lion never
left. It settled into monastery life, taking on the job of guarding the
donkey that hauled firewood from the forest each day.

The real test of the lion's loyalty came when the donkey went missing
while the lion was asleep. The other monks, already suspicious of their
new companion, assumed the lion had eaten it and refused to feed it until
it confessed by deed what it had done. Ashamed, the lion set out, found
that the donkey had merely been stolen by passing merchants, and herded
both the donkey and the merchants' camels back to the monastery, clearing
its name for good.

## A story that moved between saints

For centuries this scene has been one of the most reproduced images of
Jerome in Western art - the scholar-saint at his desk, a lion curled at
his feet. But the story itself appears to have started somewhere else
entirely: a nearly identical thorn-and-paw tale is told in the much
earlier Life of St. Gerasimus, a 5th-century monk who lived along the
Jordan River. Jerome's own voluminous surviving writings - letters,
commentaries, a famously sharp temper on display throughout - never
mention a lion at all. Somewhere in the medieval retelling of saints'
lives, Gerasimus's companion seems to have wandered into Jerome's story
and simply stayed.
