Walking the road near Bevagna, Francis of Assisi - the 13th-century friar
who founded the Franciscans - came upon a great flock of birds of
every kind gathered in the fields and trees along the way. There were
doves, crows, and others the story doesn't bother naming. Rather than pass them by, he
turned aside and greeted them as he would any crowd, then began to preach,
inviting them to praise God for the gifts they had been given: wings to
fly, feathers suited to every climate, the freedom to go wherever they
liked without needing to sow or reap. As he spoke, the birds stayed put,
stretching their necks and opening their beaks toward him, and didn't
scatter until he had finished, blessed them, and sent them on their way.

## Part of a larger pattern

The sermon to the birds is the single most reproduced scene from Francis's
life precisely because it captures something that shows up throughout the
earliest material about him, not just in later legend. Thomas of Celano,
writing a biography within a few years of Francis's 1226 death, already
describes him addressing animals as "Brother" and "Sister" and preaching
to them as fellow creatures of God. The Fioretti, compiled around a
century later, gave that general reputation its most memorable specific
scene - part of why Francis remains the Church's patron of animals and
ecology, and why parishes still hold a Blessing of the Animals in his
honor each October 4th.
