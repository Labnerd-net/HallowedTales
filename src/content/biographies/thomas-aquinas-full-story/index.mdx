Picture Roccasecca around 1225: a castle on a hill in what was then the Kingdom of Sicily, held by a minor lord named Landulf and his wife Theodora. Of their nine children, the youngest was a boy named Thomas, and because he was the youngest son, his parents had already decided what he would become before he was old enough to have an opinion about it: a monk, and eventually an abbot, at the great abbey of Monte Cassino up the road. They sent him there as a small child, five or six years old, to be raised inside its walls.

He stayed at Monte Cassino into his early teens, until war between the pope and the emperor made the abbey too dangerous a place for a boy to finish growing up in, and his family sent him instead to the university at Naples. It was there, among the merchants and students of a real city for the first time, that young Thomas met the Dominicans, a religious order only a few decades old, friars who owned nothing, begged for their bread, and preached in the streets instead of staying behind monastery walls. To a boy raised to expect a comfortable abbacy, it should have looked like a step down. Thomas, nineteen years old, decided it was exactly what he wanted, and asked to join.

His family was horrified, and did something about it. They had men intercept him on the road before he could get far and dragged him home, locking him up first at Monte San Giovanni Campano and then at the family castle in Roccasecca itself, where they kept him for the better part of a year, arguing, pleading, and waiting for him to give in. When argument failed, his brothers tried something cruder: they sent a woman into the room where he was being held, hoping she could talk him out of his vow of chastity by means that had nothing to do with talking. Thomas answered her by snatching a burning brand from the fireplace, driving her from the room, and scratching a cross into the door behind her.

What happened next, Thomas kept to himself for almost forty years, and told only once, near the end of his life, to Reginald of Piperno, the one friend he trusted with it. Alone again in the room, so he told Reginald, he fell into a kind of waking sleep, and in it two angels came to him and bound a plain cord around his waist, telling him he was being girded, on God's own authority, with a purity that would never be at risk again. He wore that cord hidden under his habit for the rest of his life and never spoke of it again until that one conversation, late in his career, with the man closest to him.

The family kept him anyway, for months longer, until the ones they had sent to break his resolve ended up changing sides instead. It was his sisters, so the story goes, who were won over first, charmed less by his arguments than by the plain fact that he would not bend, and it was they who finally helped him out, lowering him from a tower window in a basket while the household looked the other way. His sister Marotta is said to have been so struck by what she saw in him that she became a nun herself not long after. However it happened in fact, that was the end of the family's resistance. Within the year, Thomas was back with the Dominicans in Naples, and this time nobody tried to stop him.

From here the record speaks for itself, in the ledgers of universities and religious orders rather than in family stories passed down. Paris took him in 1245. In 1248 he followed his teacher, the German friar Albert the Great, to Cologne, where the other students, looking at this big, silent, slow-seeming Sicilian, nicknamed him "the dumb ox." Albert, who had seen what the others hadn't, is supposed to have told them that this ox's bellowing would one day be heard across the whole world, and he was not wrong. By 1256, Thomas held the chair of theology at the University of Paris, the single most prestigious teaching post in Christendom to be had anywhere, and he held it on and off for most of the next two decades. Somewhere around 1265, teaching now in Rome, he began the work that would define him more than any classroom ever could: the Summa Theologiae, an attempt to set the whole of Christian belief into one ordered structure, argument by argument.

By 1273 he was back in Naples, in his late forties, still at work on the Summa, writing that autumn on the subject of the Eucharist. One day he lingered in the chapel of Saint Nicholas before the large crucifix that hung near the altar, and a friar named Dominic of Caserta, nearby at the time, later said he saw what happened next: the figure on the cross seemed to speak, asking Thomas, "You have written well of me, Thomas. What reward would you have for your labor?" Thomas answered, "Nothing but you, Lord."

He never finished the Summa after that. On the sixth of December, the feast of Saint Nicholas, in the middle of saying Mass, something happened to him at the altar that he never fully explained, and he simply would not pick the pen back up. When Reginald pressed him to keep writing, Thomas told him, "I cannot, because all that I have written seems like straw to me now, compared to what I have seen." Whether that morning at Mass was the same moment as the crucifix's question, or a second experience months apart, the sources themselves do not agree. What both moments share is the same shape: a man who had given his whole adult life to writing carefully about God, finding that the writing itself had suddenly, radically stopped mattering to him.

He had only three months left. In January 1274, Pope Gregory X called him to the Council of Lyon, meant to heal the long breach between the churches of Rome and the East, and Thomas, already in poor health, set out on the journey anyway. Along the way, traveling the old Appian Road, he struck his head on a low branch, and whatever that blow did to him, on top of whatever had already been wearing him down, he never recovered from it. The monks of the Cistercian abbey at Fossanova took him in, nursed him as long as they could, and gave him the last rites. He died there on March 7, 1274, not yet fifty years old.

It did not take the Church long to agree with Albert's old prediction. Less than fifty years later, in 1323, Pope John XXII declared him a saint at Avignon, after inquiries that had gathered testimony from well over a hundred people who remembered him, Reginald of Piperno among them. In 1567 he was named a Doctor of the Church, and he has carried the old title "the Angelic Doctor" ever since, though no one has ever fully settled why "angelic" was the word that stuck, whether for the purity of his life, the clean precision of his reasoning, or simply how much of his vast output concerned the angels themselves. His bones made one more journey after his death, from Fossanova to the Church of the Jacobins in Toulouse in 1369, and it is that day of arrival, the twenty-eighth of January, that the Church keeps every year as his feast, rather than the March date of his death, which falls too often inside Lent to be kept properly.
