Francis of Assisi - the 13th-century friar who founded the Franciscan order - was born around
1181 in the hill town of Assisi, in the Umbria region of central Italy, to a prosperous cloth
merchant named Pietro Bernardone and his wife Pica. He was
baptized Giovanni, but his father, newly returned from a trading trip to France, took to calling
him Francesco instead, and the name stayed with him for the rest of his life.

As a young man, Francis lived the way his father's money allowed: fine clothes, companions, and a
reputation around Assisi for revelry. He also trained as a soldier. In 1202, when war broke out
between Assisi and the neighboring city of Perugia, Francis fought in the campaign, was taken
prisoner, and spent close to a year in a Perugian jail before his father ransomed him home.

What followed was a slow, uneven turn away from the life he had known. In his own Testament,
dictated near the end of his life, Francis wrote plainly about one encounter that marked him:
meeting a leper on the road outside Assisi, a man he would once have recoiled from, and forcing
himself to approach and embrace him instead. He named it as the moment God began leading him
toward penance. Around 1205, praying before an old Byzantine-style crucifix in the half-ruined
chapel of San Damiano outside the city walls, Francis later told his companions that he heard
Christ speak to him directly, telling him to repair his church. He took the words literally at
first, raising money to patch the chapel itself, before coming to understand them more broadly.

The break with his old life came in public and without half measures. Confronted by his furious
father before the Bishop of Assisi over money he had given away, Francis stripped off his clothes
on the spot, handed them back, and renounced his inheritance entirely. From then on he lived in
poverty by choice, begging for food, repairing chapels, and gradually drawing a small community of
followers who wanted to live the same way. By 1209 or 1210, he had enough of a following that he
traveled to Rome and received Pope Innocent III's verbal approval to preach; formal written
approval of his order's Rule came later, from Pope Honorius III in 1223.

Francis carried his vocation beyond Italy's borders. In September 1219, during the Fifth
Crusade's siege of the Egyptian city of Damietta, he crossed into the besieging army's opposite
camp with a companion, Brother Illuminato, and secured an audience with the Ayyubid sultan
al-Kamil, preaching his faith to him directly rather than through the war being fought outside
the sultan's walls. The meeting changed nothing politically; the crusade carried on regardless.
But chroniclers on both the Christian and Muslim sides recorded it as something unusual: a friar
walking unarmed into an enemy camp to talk rather than fight.

By the winter of 1223, Francis wanted Christmas told differently. At the hill town of Greccio, he
arranged a manger scene with real hay, an ox, and a donkey, and had Mass said over it in the open
air, so that the people gathered there could see the story of Christ's birth as well as hear it.
Thomas of Celano, a friar who wrote the first biography of Francis within a few years of his
death, recorded that the scene drew crowds from the surrounding countryside. The scene at Greccio
is often named as the origin of the nativity display still set up in churches and homes every
December.

His health gave way fast in his final years, worn down by travel, fasting, and, by 1224, near
blindness. In September of that year, during a forty-day retreat on the remote mountain of La
Verna, Francis is reported to have received wounds matching Christ's crucifixion in his hands,
feet, and side. Brother Leo, who was with him on the mountain and served as his confessor and
secretary, kept a small parchment Francis had written for him days later, dated the seventeenth
of September, and added his own note on the back testifying to what he had witnessed. Thomas of
Celano, drawing on Leo and the other friars who saw the wounds directly, recorded the same
account in the biography he completed a few years after Francis's death. Francis spent the rest
of his life trying to keep the wounds hidden, wrapping his hands and wearing shoes and socks he
had otherwise given up.

Nearly blind and in constant pain, Francis composed the Canticle of the Creatures in the months
before he died, a hymn praising God through the sun, moon, wind, water, and even death itself,
among the earliest surviving poems written in Italian rather than Latin. He died at the
Porziuncola, a small chapel outside Assisi, on the evening of October 3, 1226, at around
forty-four years old. Pope Gregory IX canonized him less than two years later, in July 1228, one
of the fastest canonizations in the Church's history.
