Tekakwitha was only four years old when smallpox swept through Ossernenon, the Mohawk village near where Auriesville, New York stands today, and took her father, her mother, and her baby brother within days of each other. She had been born there in 1656; she survived the epidemic, but it left her marked for the rest of her life, her face scarred and her eyesight so damaged that she spent the remainder of her days half blind, finding her way through the world mostly by feel.

An uncle, a Mohawk chief, took her in after her parents died. The village itself did not survive much longer either. French soldiers destroyed Ossernenon not long after, and its people rebuilt across the river as Gandaouagué, the same community wearing a new name. It was there, in 1675, that French Jesuit missionaries arrived to live among the Mohawk, and something in Tekakwitha, already drawn toward the faith her own mother had quietly kept, pulled her toward them. She began meeting with the priests in secret, and on Easter Sunday, 1676, she was baptized and took a new name, Catherine, said in Mohawk as Kateri.

The name cost her almost everything else. She would not work on Sundays. She turned down a marriage her uncle had already arranged for her. Neighbors who did not accept her new faith called it sorcery, children threw stones at her, and food was withheld from her at her own table. By the autumn of 1677 she had had enough of trying to hold both lives at once, and she ran. With a handful of other Christian Mohawk converts, she fled Gandaouagué for Kahnawake, a mission settlement near Montreal where other Native converts had already gathered under the Jesuits' care, nearly two hundred miles away through woods, rivers, and swamp. The journey took close to two months.

At Kahnawake she finally found the life she had been reaching for all along. She went to Mass every day, spent long hours in prayer before the Blessed Sacrament, nursed the sick and the elderly, and threw herself into fasting and penance with an intensity so severe that her own confessor eventually had to order her to stop. She took a private vow of virginity, unheard of among Mohawk women of her time, and even in failing health she carried herself, people remembered afterward, with a steady, visible joy that outlasted everything that had been done to her.

She died at Kahnawake on April 17, 1680. She was twenty-four. What happened next is the part of her story that has traveled furthest: within about a quarter of an hour of her death, according to the two Jesuit missionaries who were there, Claude Chauchetière and Pierre Cholenec, the scarring that had marked her face since childhood began to fade. By the time others had gathered around her body, her skin looked smooth and untouched, as if twenty years of visible suffering had simply been lifted off her in the space of fifteen minutes. Neither priest tried to explain it. They wrote it down the way they wrote down everything else about her, as something they had personally seen, and left it for others to make of what they would.

Their two manuscript accounts, each written independently within a couple of decades of her death, became the only real record of her life, and that same record has carried her all the way to sainthood: beatified by Pope John Paul II in 1980, canonized by Pope Benedict XVI in 2012, the first Native American to be declared a saint. Her tomb still rests beside the altar of the Saint Francis Xavier mission church at Kahnawake, where people still come to visit. Her feast is kept every July 14, and since 1939, when a North Dakota bishop first called missionaries and Native Catholics together to talk about pastoral life on the Northern Plains, an annual Tekakwitha Conference has carried her name forward every year since, Native Catholics still gathering the way the converts at Kahnawake once did.
