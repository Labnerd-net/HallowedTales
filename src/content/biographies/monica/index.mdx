Most of what's known about Monica comes from her son Augustine, who set her life down in his
Confessions years after her death, remembering her less as a record to get straight than as
someone he was still working out how to thank.

She grew up in a Christian household in Thagaste, a town in Roman North Africa, born around the
year 331. The faith she learned there would carry her through a marriage, a widowhood, and a long
argument with her own son that she refused to let go of.

Her husband, Patricius, wasn't a Christian for most of their marriage. He was generous enough with
friends, Augustine would later recall, but difficult at home - quick-tempered, unfaithful. Monica
learned to pick her moments, waiting out his anger rather than meeting it, and prayed for the
rest.

They had three children. Augustine, born in 354, was the one whose education his parents invested
in most, hoping it would carry him somewhere his father never reached. There was also a son,
Navigius, and a daughter whose name didn't survive in the record.

Patricius became a Christian before he died - one of his wife's quieter victories, by Augustine's
telling. It left her a widow, with the harder work of raising Augustine still ahead of her. That
work was about to get harder still, because the education she and Patricius had wanted so badly
for him was starting to carry him somewhere she couldn't follow.

As a young man studying in Carthage, a major city in Roman North Africa, Augustine joined the
Manicheans, a sect well outside the Catholic faith. Monica took it hard enough that she stopped
letting him eat at her table for a time.

Then, by Augustine's account, she had a dream. She was standing on a wooden rule, grieving, when a
young man told her not to be troubled and pointed out that Augustine was standing beside her. When
she described it to her son, he suggested - ever his mother's opponent in argument - that maybe
she'd come around to his beliefs instead. She wasn't having it: the dream had put him next to her,
not the other way around.

She let him back to her table and kept looking for someone who could reach him. She found a bishop
and pressed him to sit down and argue her son out of his error. He told her, more or less, to leave
it alone - a young man that pleased with his own cleverness wouldn't be talked out of anything, and
she should pray and wait instead. She pressed until he said, mostly to get her to stop: the son of
a mother who cried the way she cried wasn't going to be lost.

She held onto that one line for years before she had any other reason to believe it.

By 383, Augustine was teaching in Carthage and planning a move to Rome, hoping for better
conditions for his work. Monica went with him as far as the harbor, wanting him either to stay or
to take her along. He told her he was only seeing a friend off, then talked her into spending the
night praying at a chapel of Saint Cyprian, a third-century bishop and martyr of Carthage, nearby.
While she was there, he boarded a ship and left without her.

She found out what he'd done the next morning. Augustine, writing it down years later, didn't
spare himself the detail that he'd lied to his own mother to get away. Monica had prayed God would
keep him from making that exact trip; the prayer went unanswered, at least as she'd framed it.
What she did next, by his account, was go back to her routine - alms, church morning and evening -
and keep praying anyway.

She eventually crossed the sea herself and found him in Milan, a city in northern Italy where he
was teaching by then. He'd left the Manicheans but hadn't become a Catholic. She told him, with a
confidence Augustine remembered clearly, that she expected to see him believe before she died.

In Milan she grew attached to the church led by Bishop Ambrose, whose preaching was quietly
working on Augustine's resistance to Scripture. Monica listened too, and when she learned Ambrose
had forbidden a devotional custom she'd brought with her from Africa, she gave it up without much
fuss - a willingness to be corrected that her son noticed and wrote down.

She also tried to arrange a proper marriage for him, on the theory that it might finally bring him
to baptism. The engagement went ahead; the woman Augustine had been living with for years went
back to Africa, though their son, Adeodatus, stayed with him. The marriage itself never happened.
Whatever Monica had hoped to settle by arranging it, her son's situation wasn't getting settled on
her schedule.

It settled instead in a garden in 386, where Augustine was sitting with his friend Alypius when he
broke down in tears, heard what sounded like a child telling him to take up and read, opened a book
of Paul's letters at random, and found the end of an argument he'd been having with himself for
years.

He and Alypius went inside and told Monica what had happened. She rejoiced - Augustine's word for
it - at hearing that her son had decided to give himself to God and give up on marriage
altogether. It wasn't the resolution she'd spent years arranging, but it went further than she'd
gotten on her own.

At Easter in 387, Ambrose baptized Augustine, along with Alypius and Adeodatus, in Milan. Monica
had lived to see it. Later that year the household started back toward Africa together, getting
as far as the port town of Ostia before stopping to wait for a ship.

It was there, by a window looking out on a garden, that Monica and Augustine had the conversation
he would later try hardest to put into words - about eternal life, reaching, by his account,
toward something neither of them could fully say.

Not long after, she came down with a fever. She'd once wanted to be buried beside her husband back
in Africa. Now, she told her sons, it didn't matter where her body ended up - only that they
remember her at the altar, wherever they happened to be.

She died at Ostia in 387, around fifty-six years old, and was buried there. Augustine kept her
memory the way she'd asked: by writing her into the Confessions, and closing the account of her
life by asking anyone reading it to pray for her too.
