Nicholas - the 4th-century bishop who, centuries later and an ocean away, became the root figure
behind Santa Claus - is, by the honest admission of his own earliest biographers, almost a
historical blank. He was born in Patara, a city on the Lycian coast of what is now southern
Turkey, and in time became bishop of nearby Myra. Beyond that, almost nothing about his life can
be called certain. The traditional birth year sometimes given for him, 270, is a modern
convenience rather than an ancient record; no source from his own lifetime survives at all. The
earliest full account of his life was not written until the 9th century, by a monk known as
Michael the Archimandrite, roughly five hundred years after Nicholas is believed to have lived.
Michael's own contemporary, the Byzantine patriarch Methodius, said as much directly: up to that
point, he wrote, the life of this "distinguished shepherd" had remained largely unknown to the
faithful.

Two claims repeated across those early Greek lives are worth naming honestly rather than
asserting as fact. The first is that Nicholas was imprisoned for his faith during the persecution
of Christians carried out under the emperor Diocletian in the early 4th century, and that he made
what the old accounts call "a glorious confession" rather than renounce it. The second is that he
later attended the Council of Nicaea in 325, the landmark gathering of bishops that condemned the
teaching of a priest named Arius, who held that Christ was a created being rather than co-eternal
with God the Father. Both claims appear consistently in the Greek tradition about Nicholas, and
both are undermined by the same gap: no surviving document from the 4th century itself, including
the Council's own attendance records, mentions him by name. Historians have long treated both as
traditional rather than confirmed.

That gap in the record is also exactly where a much later and far more dramatic story took root.
Popular retellings often have Nicholas, provoked beyond patience by Arius's heresy at Nicaea,
crossing the council floor and slapping him. It is a vivid scene, and it has
become genuinely embedded in some corners of Christian tradition; the Eastern Orthodox
iconographic manual used by generations of icon painters eventually included it as a standard
scene. But the story cannot be traced back anywhere near Nicholas's own century, or even close to
it. Its earliest known appearance is in a 14th-century text by Petrus de Natalibus, and even there
the man struck is only an unnamed "Arian," not Arius himself. It was not until a 16th-century
account by Damaskenos Monachos that the story was rewritten with Arius named outright. Between
Nicaea itself and that first telling lie roughly a thousand years of silence, long enough that the
episode belongs to the history of how Nicholas was remembered, rather than to any record of what
he actually did.

Nicholas is remembered far more gently through a different story, one with a real textual trail
behind it: the night he secretly provided dowry gold for three sisters whose father could not
afford to see them married. That story, and the chimney and stocking variations that grew out of
it over time, is its own telling elsewhere; what matters here is only that it, too, traces back to
Michael's 9th-century life, the same source as the Nicaea material, underscoring how much of what
is "known" about Nicholas was first written down centuries after he died.

What can be dated with real confidence is not Nicholas's life but what happened to his remains
afterward. He died, by tradition, on December 6 in the year 343, the date kept ever since as his
feast day, and was buried in Myra. His tomb stayed there for centuries, drawing pilgrims, until the
city fell under Muslim rule following its capture by Seljuk Turkish forces in the 11th century.
In 1087, a
group of sailors from the Italian port city of Bari arranged to remove Nicholas's relics from Myra
and carry them home, where they were received as a civic and religious triumph; they remain
enshrined today in the Basilica di San Nicola in Bari, which is how the saint sometimes acquired
the alternate title "Nicholas of Bari." Venice, Bari's maritime rival, separately claimed to have
recovered other remains of his around the same period, a rivalry the historical record preserves
honestly rather than resolves. It is a strange shape for a life to take: almost nothing certain
about the man while he lived, and then, thirteen centuries after the fact, two Italian cities
competing over where exactly he would rest.
