Thomas Aquinas - the medieval theologian and Dominican friar best known for the *Summa
Theologiae* - was born around 1225 at Roccasecca, the youngest of nine children of a minor lord
named Landulf and his wife Theodora. As the youngest son, he was given to the great abbey of Monte
Cassino as a child oblate, at five or six years old, in the hope that he'd grow up to become its
abbot. He stayed there into his early teens, until fighting between the pope and the emperor made
the abbey too dangerous a place to stay, and he was sent to continue his education at the
university in Naples instead.

It was in Naples that he met the Dominicans, a mendicant order only a few decades old, and decided
at nineteen to join them rather than return to Monte Cassino as his family expected. They were
furious. A begging friar who owned nothing looked like a step down from an abbot, not up, and they
had him intercepted on the road and held for the better part of a year at their own castles,
trying to talk him out of it. At one point, according to the testimony gathered later, his
brothers sent a woman into the room to tempt him from his vow of chastity; he drove her out with a
burning brand from the fireplace and marked the door with a cross. What happened next, by his own
account, was a vision: left alone in the room, he said two angels came and bound a cord around his
waist, telling him he was being girded, by God's own authority, with a purity that would never
again be at risk. He's said to have worn that cord under his habit for the rest of his life, and
kept the story to himself for nearly forty years, telling it only once, near the end, to his
closest companion.

His family held out a while longer, until, by tradition, it was his sisters who were won over and
helped him escape through a tower window in a basket. One of them, Marotta, is said to have been
so moved by it that she became a nun herself. However it actually happened, the resistance ended
there, and within the year he was back with the Dominicans in Naples.

Paris took him in 1245; in 1248 he followed his
teacher, the German Dominican Albert the Great, to Cologne, where his fellow students, unimpressed
by his size and his silence, nicknamed him the "dumb ox" - a nickname Albert is said to have
answered by predicting that this ox's teaching would one day be heard across the world. By 1256 he
held the chair of theology at Paris, a post he kept on and off until 1272, with a gap in the
middle rather than one long unbroken tenure. Around 1265, teaching now in Rome, he began the work
that would define him more than any classroom: the *Summa Theologiae*, a systematic attempt to
lay out the whole of Christian belief.

He never finished it. On December 6, 1273, while saying Mass in Naples, something happened to him
that he never explained, and he simply stopped writing. When his companion Reginald of Piperno
pressed him to go on, Thomas told him that everything he'd written now seemed like straw to him.
The best-known version of that period has him kneeling before a crucifix in the chapel of Saint
Nicholas not long before, where a friar named Dominic of Caserta later testified he heard the
figure on the cross ask Thomas what reward he wanted for his writing, and Thomas answer, "Nothing
but you, Lord" - a scene that rests on that one friar's testimony, offered as part of the same
canonization record as the rest.

In January 1274, Pope Gregory X summoned him to the Council of Lyon. He set out despite failing
health, and struck his head on a low branch along the way, by one account. He was taken to the
Cistercian abbey at Fossanova, where he died on March 7, 1274, not yet fifty.

Less than fifty years later, in 1323, Pope John XXII canonized him at Avignon, after inquiries
that gathered testimony from well over a hundred people who had known him. In 1567 he was named a
Doctor of the Church, and has been called the Angelic Doctor ever since, for reasons no one has
ever fully settled - the purity of his life, the precision of his reasoning, or simply how much he
wrote about angels. His relics made one more journey, from Fossanova to Toulouse in 1369, and it's
that date, not his March death date, which falls inside Lent too often to be kept properly, that
the Church now observes as his feast.

## A life told in two kinds of record

Thomas's schooling, his teaching posts, the dates of his major works, and the circumstances of his
death are documented about as well as a medieval scholar's can be - recorded independently, in
university and Dominican-order sources, of anyone's opinion of his character. The more personal
moments - his family's year-long attempt to talk him out of the Dominicans, the vision of the
cord, the crucifix's question - survive for a different reason entirely: testimony gathered
decades later, when the Church was deciding whether to canonize him. Without that process, and
without Reginald of Piperno's willingness, late in his own life, to repeat what his friend had
confided to him just once, none of it would have come down to us at all.
