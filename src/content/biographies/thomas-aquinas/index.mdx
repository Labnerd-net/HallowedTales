Thomas Aquinas was born around 1225 at Roccasecca, a hill town in what was then the Kingdom of Sicily and is now the Italian region of Lazio, the youngest of nine children of Landulf, lord of Roccasecca, and his wife Theodora. As the family's youngest son, Thomas was given to the great Benedictine abbey of Monte Cassino as a child oblate at about five or six years old, in the hope that he would grow up to become its abbot, a position that would have brought real standing to the family. He stayed at the abbey until his early teens, when fighting between Emperor Frederick II and Pope Gregory IX disrupted monastic life there, and he was sent instead to continue his education at the University of Naples.

It was at Naples that Thomas encountered the Dominicans, the still-young order of mendicant friars founded only a few decades earlier, and decided, at around nineteen, to join them rather than return to Monte Cassino as his family expected. His family was furious. Rather than the quiet dignity of a Benedictine abbot, Thomas was proposing to become a wandering friar who owned nothing and begged for his bread, a step down, in his family's eyes, not up. They had him intercepted on the road and held him for close to a year at their own castles of Monte San Giovanni Campano and Roccasecca, trying to talk him out of it. At one point, hoping a more direct approach would work, his brothers sent a woman into his room to tempt him from his vow of chastity; Thomas drove her out with a burning brand pulled from the fireplace and marked the door with a cross. What happened immediately afterward, by his own account late in life to his closest companion, is its own story, told in full elsewhere. By tradition, it was ultimately his sisters, sent to help talk him down, who were won over to his side instead and who helped him escape the tower by lowering him from a window in a basket; his eldest sister Marotta is said to have been so moved by their conversations that she became a nun herself. However it actually happened, the family relented, and Thomas returned to the Dominicans at Naples within the year.

From there his life becomes considerably easier to document, since it runs through university and religious-order records rather than family memory. In 1245 he was sent to study in Paris, and in 1248 he followed his teacher, the German Dominican Albert the Great, to Cologne. Thomas was a large, quiet young man, and his fellow students nicknamed him the "dumb ox," a nickname Albert is said to have turned on its head, predicting that this same ox's teaching "will one day be heard throughout the world." By 1256 Thomas held a regent master's chair in theology at the University of Paris, the most prestigious teaching post in Christian Europe, a position he held until 1259 and again from 1268 to 1272. Around 1265, while teaching in Rome, he began what would become his best-known work, the *Summa Theologiae*, a systematic treatment of Christian doctrine still studied today, left unfinished at his death.

That work stopped abruptly on December 6, 1273, the feast of Saint Nicholas, while Thomas was celebrating Mass at the Dominican convent in Naples. Something happened to him there, though he never fully explained what, after which he simply would not write anymore. When his companion and confessor, Reginald of Piperno, pressed him to continue, Thomas answered, "I cannot, because all that I have written seems like straw to me." The most famous telling of that morning, involving a crucifix that is said to have spoken to him, is told as its own legend elsewhere; what is certain is that the *Summa* stayed unfinished, and that Thomas himself considered whatever he had seen to outweigh everything he had written.

He did not live much longer after that. In January 1274, Pope Gregory X summoned him to attend the Second Council of Lyon, called to work toward reunion between the Eastern and Western churches. Thomas set out on the journey but fell ill along the way, reportedly after striking his head on a low branch along the Appian Way, the ancient Roman road he was traveling, a detail some modern writers have connected to a theory that he suffered a slow-developing head injury, though that remains a retrospective guess rather than anything a 13th-century observer could have diagnosed. He was taken to the nearby Cistercian abbey of Fossanova, where he received the last rites and died on March 7, 1274, not yet fifty years old.

Less than fifty years later, on July 18, 1323, Pope John XXII canonized him at Avignon, following two formal inquiries that gathered testimony from well over a hundred people who had known him. In 1567 Pope Pius V named him a Doctor of the Church, and he is still known today by the old title "Doctor Angelicus," the Angelic Doctor, though exactly why "angelic" is the word that stuck is less settled than the title itself: some point to the subtlety of his reasoning, others to the purity of his life, others simply to how much he wrote about angels, and no single explanation has ever been treated as the final word. His relics, moved from Fossanova to the Church of the Jacobins in Toulouse on January 28, 1369, remain there today, and it is that date of translation, not his March death date that regularly falls within Lent, which the Church now keeps each year as his feast.
