Picture Roccasecca around 1225: a castle on a hill in what was then the Kingdom of Sicily, held by a minor lord named Landulf and his wife Theodora. Of their nine children, the youngest was a boy named Thomas, and because he was the youngest son, his parents had already decided what he would become before he was old enough to have an opinion about it: a monk, and eventually an abbot, at the great abbey of Monte Cassino up the road. They sent him there as a small child, five or six years old, to be raised inside its walls.

He stayed at Monte Cassino into his early teens, until war between the pope and the emperor made the abbey too dangerous a place for a boy to finish growing up in, and his family sent him instead to the university at Naples. It was there, among the merchants and students of a real city for the first time, that young Thomas met the Dominicans.

The Dominicans were something new: a religious order only a few decades old, friars who owned nothing, begged for their bread, and preached in the streets instead of staying behind monastery walls. To a boy raised to expect a comfortable abbacy, it should have looked like a step down. Thomas, nineteen years old, decided it was exactly what he wanted, and asked to join.

His family was horrified, and did something about it. They had men intercept him on the road before he could get far and dragged him home, locking him up first at Monte San Giovanni Campano and then at the family castle in Roccasecca itself, where they kept him for the better part of a year, arguing, pleading, and waiting for him to give in. When argument failed, his brothers tried something cruder: they sent a woman into the room where he was being held, hoping she could talk him out of his vow of chastity by means that had nothing to do with talking. Thomas answered her by snatching a burning brand from the fireplace, driving her from the room, and scratching a cross into the door behind her. What he saw or felt in the silence that followed, he would only describe once, years later, to the one friend he trusted with it, and that moment has become its own legend.

The family kept him anyway, for months longer, until the ones they had sent to break his resolve ended up changing sides instead. It was his sisters, so the story goes, who were won over first, charmed less by his arguments than by the plain fact that he would not bend, and it was they who finally helped him out, lowering him from a tower window in a basket while the household looked the other way. His sister Marotta is said to have been so struck by what she saw in him that she became a nun herself not long after. However it happened in fact, that was the end of the family's resistance. Within the year, Thomas was back with the Dominicans in Naples, and this time nobody tried to stop him.

From here the record speaks for itself, in the ledgers of universities and religious orders rather than in family stories passed down. Paris took him in 1245. In 1248 he followed his teacher, the German friar Albert the Great, to Cologne, where the other students, looking at this big, silent, slow-seeming Sicilian, nicknamed him "the dumb ox." Albert, who had seen what the others hadn't, is supposed to have told them that this ox's bellowing would one day be heard across the whole world, and he was not wrong. By 1256, Thomas held the chair of theology at the University of Paris, the single most prestigious teaching post in Christendom to be had anywhere, and he held it on and off for most of the next two decades. Somewhere around 1265, teaching now in Rome, he began the work that would define him more than any classroom ever could: the Summa Theologiae, an attempt to set the whole of Christian belief into one ordered structure, argument by argument. He never finished it.

He stopped on the sixth of December, 1273, in the middle of saying Mass at the Dominican house in Naples. Something happened to him at the altar that morning. He never said exactly what. He only came back changed, and simply would not pick the pen back up. His companion Reginald begged him to keep writing. Thomas told him, "I cannot, because all that I have written seems like straw to me now, compared to what I have seen." The story that has grown up around that morning says a crucifix spoke to him from the altar; what is certain is only that the Summa stopped there, three-quarters built, and that the man who had just finished saying Mass thought the whole of it weighed less than whatever he had glimpsed.

He had only three months left. In January 1274, Pope Gregory X called him to the Council of Lyon, meant to heal the long breach between the churches of Rome and the East, and Thomas, already in poor health, set out on the journey anyway. Along the way, traveling the old Appian Road, he struck his head on a low branch, and whatever that blow did to him, on top of whatever had already been wearing him down, he never recovered from it. The monks of the Cistercian abbey at Fossanova took him in, nursed him as long as they could, and gave him the last rites. He died there on March 7, 1274, not yet fifty years old.

It did not take the Church long to agree with Albert's old prediction. Less than fifty years later, in 1323, Pope John XXII declared him a saint at Avignon, after inquiries that had gathered testimony from well over a hundred people who remembered him. In 1567 he was named a Doctor of the Church, and he has carried the old title "the Angelic Doctor" ever since, though no one has ever fully settled why "angelic" was the word that stuck, whether for the purity of his life, the clean precision of his reasoning, or simply how much of his vast output concerned the angels themselves. His bones made one more journey after his death, from Fossanova to the Church of the Jacobins in Toulouse in 1369, and it is that day of arrival, the twenty-eighth of January, that the Church keeps every year as his feast, rather than the March date of his death, which falls too often inside Lent to be kept properly.
