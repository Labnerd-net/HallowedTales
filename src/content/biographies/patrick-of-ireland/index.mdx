Patrick - the 5th-century missionary who became the patron saint of Ireland - left behind two
pieces of his own writing, a short spiritual autobiography called the Confessio and a letter
denouncing the slave trade called the Epistola ad Milites Corotici. Between them, they are
almost everything reliably known about his life. In the Confessio, Patrick names his birthplace
as a place called Bannavem Taburniae, somewhere in Roman Britain, though no one has ever
securely matched that name to a known site. He gives no exact birth year either; historians
place it somewhere in the early-to-mid 5th century. He does name his family: his father
Calpornius held the Roman civic office of decurion and was also a deacon, and his grandfather
Potitus had been a priest before him.

At sixteen, Patrick was captured by Irish raiders and carried off into slavery in Ireland. He
spent six years there, by his own account largely alone in the wilderness tending sheep, cut off
from the Christian household he had grown up in. Later tradition, though not Patrick's own
writing, places this captivity near a mountain called Slemish, in what is now County Antrim,
under a local chieftain named Miliucc. Patrick wrote that the isolation deepened rather than
broke his faith: left with nothing else, he turned to constant prayer, and came to feel that his
belief had grown stronger through the ordeal than it had ever been at home.

His escape, in his own telling, began with a vision: he understood that a ship was waiting for
him on the coast, roughly two hundred miles from where he was being held. He made the journey,
talked his way aboard despite having nothing to offer the crew, and eventually made it back to
his family in Britain. He might reasonably have stayed there. Instead, Patrick wrote that he had
a second vision not long after his return: a figure he names as Victoricus appeared to him
carrying a letter headed "The Voice of the Irish," calling him back to the country where he had
been enslaved, this time as a missionary rather than a captive.

What happened between that vision and his return to Ireland is where Patrick's own writing runs
out and later biography takes over. Tradition holds that he spent years training on the
European mainland, with accounts associating him with the great cathedral school at Auxerre,
before being consecrated a bishop and sent back to Ireland, traditionally dated to the year 432.
Once there, the fullest and earliest account of his mission, a Life written by a monk named
Muirchu around the year 680, roughly two centuries after Patrick's own lifetime, describes a
direct confrontation with the island's pagan establishment. By Muirchu's telling, Patrick lit a
Paschal fire for Easter on the Hill of Slane on the same night the high king's druids held a
fire ban in place at Tara, seat of Ireland's high king, until the king's own fire was lit first.
Patrick's fire, visible from Tara, was taken by the druids as a challenge they could not undo
once it had been set alight. The confrontation that followed, as Muirchu tells it, ended with
the king's own household converting. It is a story carried by a source writing generations after
the fact, not an eyewitness account, but it has shaped how Patrick's mission is remembered ever
since.

Patrick's missionary life also included a forty-day fast on the mountain now called Croagh
Patrick, in County Mayo. Equally well attested, if less dramatic, is the practical work of
building a church across Ireland: establishing Armagh, traditionally founded around 444, as the
island's leading ecclesiastical center, and organizing scattered Christian communities into
something more lasting.

One more piece of Patrick's legacy deserves an honest note on its origin. The prayer known today
as "Saint Patrick's Breastplate," or the Lorica, a hymn invoking Christ's protection "before me,
behind me, in me, in quiet, in danger," is widely attributed to him and still sung and prayed in
his name. But the earliest surviving copies date only to the 11th century, and the language of
the prayer itself is judged on linguistic grounds to belong to around the 8th century - three
hundred years after Patrick lived. The attribution is a long and sincere tradition, not a
documented fact, and the prayer's popularity hasn't needed the distinction to survive this long.

Patrick's death is traditionally dated to March 17, the date his feast is still kept, though the
year itself is disputed in the old annals between 461 and 493, and no modern scholarly consensus
has settled it. Tradition holds that he was buried at Downpatrick, a site that has drawn
pilgrims for well over a thousand years regardless of which year is correct. What survives most
solidly of Patrick, in the end, is not the legend of the fire at Slane or the prayer that carries
his name, but his own two short pieces of writing: a man recounting, in his own words, what it
was to be taken as a slave to a country he would later choose to return to and spend his life
serving.
