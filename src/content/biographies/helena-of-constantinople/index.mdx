Helena - mother of the Roman emperor Constantine, later venerated as a saint in her own right -
was born around the middle of the third century, probably in the town of Drepanum in Bithynia, a
Roman province in what is now northwestern Turkey. Constantine later renamed the town Helenopolis
in her honor. Her origins were humble enough that the bishop Ambrose of Milan, writing a century
later, called her a "stabularia" - a stable-keeper's or innkeeper's daughter - a detail some
historians read as a genuine memory of where she came from and others read as hostile gossip
aimed at a woman who had risen to become an empress.

Around the year 270, Helena met a Roman military officer named Constantius, likely during one of
his postings in Asia Minor. Ancient sources disagree on the exact legal status of their
relationship, calling her his wife in some accounts and his concubine in others. Whatever its
standing, the union produced a son: the future emperor Constantine, born on February 27, probably
in the year 272, at Naissus in what is now Serbia. The arrangement did not last. Before 289,
Constantius divorced Helena to marry Theodora, stepdaughter of the reigning emperor Maximian, a
match built for political advantage rather than affection. Helena never remarried, and for years
afterward she lived away from public life.

Her fortunes changed entirely once her son's did. By 312, Constantine held power, and Helena
returned to prominence at court, eventually receiving the title Augusta, or empress, in 324. The
historian Eusebius of Caesarea, writing within a few years of her death, records that Constantine
gave his mother unrestricted access to the imperial treasury, wealth she used in part to fund
churches across the empire.

Around 326 to 328, in her late seventies, she traveled to the Holy Land. Eusebius describes her
funding or enriching the Church of the Nativity in Bethlehem and a church on the Mount of Olives,
and founding additional churches in the eastern provinces beyond Jerusalem itself.

What Eusebius never mentions is the story most people now associate with her name. By a tradition
that surfaces only a generation later, in a funeral oration Ambrose delivered for the emperor
Theodosius in 395 and in a church history the historian Rufinus of Aquileia wrote around 402, Helena used this
same journey to excavate the site of the crucifixion, uncovering three buried crosses and
identifying the true one by having it touch a dying woman, who was healed where the other two had
failed. The same two accounts credit her with two more relics from the same journey: a marble
staircase said to be the one Christ climbed before Pilate, later carried to Rome, and a seamless
tunic said to have been sent on to Trier.

Helena died around the year 330, in Rome, with Constantine at her side. She was buried in a
mausoleum built for her along the Via Labicana outside the city, and tradition holds that her
remains were later moved, in the twelfth century, to the church of Santa Maria in Aracoeli in
Rome, where devotion to her has continued since. Her feast is kept on August 18.

## A pilgrimage recorded, a legend added two generations later

Eusebius, writing within a few years of Helena's own death, is close enough to her lifetime to
make the pilgrimage and the church-building the most solidly attested part of her story. The three
relics rest on Ambrose and Rufinus instead, writing roughly seventy years after she died - no one
closer to her own time mentions any of them. The staircase goes unmentioned in any surviving
record for over a thousand years after that, until pilgrims are documented climbing it at the 1450
Jubilee, and the charter meant to prove the tunic's journey from her hands is generally considered
a later forgery. However old and however loved the relic traditions have since become, her feast
marks the life Eusebius recorded: a woman of uncertain, possibly humble birth who outlived a
political marriage, waited years in obscurity, and then spent the wealth and the old age her son's
power gave her funding churches in the land where her faith had taken root.
