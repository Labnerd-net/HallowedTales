Helena - mother of the Roman emperor Constantine, later venerated as a saint in her own right -
was born around the middle of the third century, probably in the town of Drepanum in Bithynia, a
Roman province in what is now northwestern Turkey. Constantine later renamed the town Helenopolis
in her honor. Her origins were humble enough that the bishop Ambrose of Milan, writing a century
later, called her a "stabularia" - a stable-keeper's or innkeeper's daughter - a detail some
historians read as a genuine memory of where she came from and others read as hostile gossip
aimed at a woman who had risen to become an empress.

Around the year 270, Helena met a Roman military officer named Constantius, likely during one of
his postings in Asia Minor. Ancient sources disagree on the exact legal status of their
relationship, calling her his wife in some accounts and his concubine in others. Whatever its
standing, the union produced a son: the future emperor Constantine, born on February 27, probably
in the year 272, at Naissus in what is now Serbia. The arrangement did not last. Before 289,
Constantius divorced Helena to marry Theodora, stepdaughter of the reigning emperor Maximian, a
match built for political advantage rather than affection. Helena never remarried, and for years
afterward she lived away from public life.

Her fortunes changed entirely once her son's did. By 312, Constantine held power, and Helena
returned to prominence at court, eventually receiving the title Augusta, or empress, in 324. The
historian Eusebius of Caesarea, writing within a few years of her death, records that Constantine
gave his mother unrestricted access to the imperial treasury, wealth she used in part to fund
churches across the empire.

The best-documented chapter of Helena's life is a journey she made late in it. Around 326 to 328,
in her late seventies, she traveled to the Holy Land. Eusebius describes her funding or enriching
the Church of the Nativity in Bethlehem and a church on the Mount of Olives, and founding
additional churches in the eastern provinces beyond Jerusalem itself. This part of her story rests
on a source close to her own lifetime, which makes it the most solidly attested thing she is known
to have done.

What Eusebius does not mention is the story most people now associate with her name: that during
this same journey, Helena excavated the site of the crucifixion and uncovered three buried
crosses. To identify the true one, she had it touch a dying woman, who was restored to health
when only that cross worked where the other two had failed. That account appears nowhere in
Eusebius's near-contemporary record. It surfaces only a generation later, in a funeral oration
Ambrose delivered for the emperor Theodosius in 395, and in a church history written around 402
by the historian Rufinus of Aquileia - both roughly seventy years after Helena's own death.
However old and however deeply loved the story has since become, it is a later addition to her
biography, not something recorded by anyone who witnessed her actual journey.

The same pattern repeats for two more relics tradition credits to this same journey: a marble
staircase said to be the one Christ climbed before Pontius Pilate, later carried to Rome, and a
seamless tunic said to have been sent to Trier, in Germany. Neither story fares any better under
scrutiny than the True Cross does. The stairs go undocumented for over a thousand years, only
clearly attested as an object of pilgrim devotion at the 1450 Jubilee; the tunic's supporting
charter is generally considered a later forgery rather than a genuine record from Helena's own
lifetime.

Helena died around the year 330, in Rome, with Constantine at her side. She was buried in a
mausoleum built for her along the Via Labicana outside the city. Tradition holds that her remains
were later moved, in the twelfth century, to the church of Santa Maria in Aracoeli in Rome, where
devotion to her has continued since. Her feast is kept on August 18, marking not the legend that
made her famous, but the life that came before it: a woman of uncertain and possibly humble birth
who outlived a political marriage, waited years in obscurity, and spent the wealth and the old age
her son's power gave her funding churches in the land where her faith had taken root.
