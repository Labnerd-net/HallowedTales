Picture the Roman world around the middle of the third century, in Drepanum, a town in Bithynia on what is now Turkey's northwestern coast. Constantine would later rename it Helenopolis, in honor of the girl born there named Helena. Her own son's power would eventually rewrite how the world saw her, but a bishop writing a century later, not altogether kindly, called her a "stabularia," a stable-keeper's daughter, a detail some read as an honest memory of modest birth and others as hostile gossip about a woman who had risen far above where she started.

Around the year 270, she met a Roman officer named Constantius, probably during one of his postings through Asia Minor. Ancient writers disagree on whether she was his wife or his concubine, but the relationship produced a son, born around 272 at Naissus, in what is now Serbia, who would grow up to become the emperor Constantine. It did not last. Before 289, Constantius divorced her to marry Theodora, stepdaughter of the reigning emperor, a match built for advantage rather than affection, and Helena spent years afterward living away from public life, with nothing of her former position left to her.

Then her son's fortunes changed, and so, almost overnight, did hers. By 312, Constantine held real power, and his mother returned to the center of things, receiving the title Augusta, empress, in 324. Constantine gave her free use of the imperial treasury, and she spent part of it funding churches across the empire.

The best-documented chapter of her life came at its very end. In her late seventies, around 326, she traveled to the Holy Land, and the historian Eusebius, writing within a few years of her death, describes her funding the Church of the Nativity in Bethlehem, a church on the Mount of Olives, and more besides across the eastern provinces. That much is close to certain, recorded by someone writing near enough to her own lifetime to have heard it firsthand.

What Eusebius never mentions is the part of the story everyone remembers her for now. Tradition holds that during this same journey, she had the site of the crucifixion excavated and found three buried crosses beneath it, with no way to tell which one had held Christ. To settle it, she had the crosses brought to a dying woman, one at a time: two did nothing, and the third restored her to health on the spot. Helena divided the relic, tradition says, leaving part of it in Jerusalem and carrying the rest home to Rome, where it rests today at the basilica of Santa Croce in Gerusalemme, built, by tradition, specifically to house it. That story appears nowhere in Eusebius's near-contemporary record; it surfaces only a generation later, in a funeral oration Ambrose gave for the emperor Theodosius in 395 and in a church history Rufinus of Aquileia wrote around 402, both roughly seventy years after Helena's own death. Churches across medieval Europe would go on claiming fragments of that same cross for centuries afterward, until pieces of the True Cross could be found in hundreds of places at once, a relic so holy, people came to believe, that it could be divided endlessly without ever running out.

By the time she died in Rome, around the year 330, with Constantine at her side, two more relics had quietly attached themselves to the same journey: a marble staircase, said to be the very steps Christ climbed before Pilate, which tradition says she carried to Rome, and a seamless tunic, said to have been sent on afterward to Trier. Neither claim can be traced back any further than she herself can be traced forward. The staircase goes unmentioned in any record for over a thousand years, until pilgrims are documented climbing it at the 1450 Jubilee, and the charter meant to prove the tunic's journey from her hands is generally considered a later forgery. Both relics are still venerated today, but neither one ever grew into a scene of its own the way the finding of the cross did; they travel with her name as an afterthought, not a story.

She was buried outside Rome, along the Via Labicana, and tradition holds that her remains were later moved, in the twelfth century, to the church of Santa Maria in Aracoeli, where devotion to her continues. Her feast falls on August 18, and whatever else attached itself to her name in the centuries after her death, it marks the life that came first: a woman of uncertain, possibly humble birth, who outlived a political marriage, waited years in obscurity, and then spent the wealth and the old age her son's power gave her doing something that needed no legend to justify it, funding churches in the land where her faith had taken root.
