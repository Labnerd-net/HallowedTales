## Feast-day customs

- **St. Lucy's Day (Dec 13)** — in Scandinavia, a daughter wears a crown of candles and leads a
  procession of light; saffron "Lucia buns" are baked. Ties to her name (Lucia = light) and
  the darkest point of the old Julian calendar year.
- **St. Joseph's Day (Mar 19)** — St. Joseph's Table/Altar tradition (Sicilian-American),
  elaborate food displays shared with the community; fava beans kept as "lucky beans" tied to
  a legend of the bean crop surviving a famine when other crops failed; zeppole di San
  Giuseppe pastries.
- **St. Martin's Day / Martinmas (Nov 11)** — children's lantern processions in parts of
  Europe; goose dinners, tied to a legend that Martin hid in a goose pen to avoid being made
  bishop and was given away by the geese's noise.
- **St. Agnes' Eve (Jan 20–21)** — old folk custom that a young woman performing certain
  rituals would see her future husband in a dream; mostly survives today through Keats' poem
  rather than active practice. Flag: closest thing on this list to "folk magic" — tell as
  literary/historical curiosity, not as a how-to.
- **Candlemas (Feb 2)** — blessing of candles for the year, candlelit procession; European
  weather-lore link to Groundhog Day (a badger/hedgehog predicting weather in older European
  versions, later becoming the US groundhog).

## Liturgical-object traditions

- **Candlemas candles** — household candles blessed for the year, historically kept on hand for
  storms/sickness/comfort at a deathbed.

## Food traditions

(Flagged by the user as a favorite angle — good candidate to grow into its own file once this
list gets long enough.)

- **King Cake (Epiphany / Mardi Gras season)** — hidden bean or baby figurine baked inside;
  whoever finds it has a role the following year (luck, or hosting duties, depending on
  region). Ties to the Magi's visit on Epiphany.
- **Pretzels in Lent** — traditional shape said to represent arms crossed in prayer; original
  recipe (flour, water, salt only) matched strict Lenten fasting rules.
- **Fish on Fridays** — the abstinence-from-meat practice and its folk knock-on effects (the
  "Friday fish fry" as a cultural institution, especially in the US Midwest).
- **Feast of the Seven Fishes (Christmas Eve, Italian-American)** — elaborate multi-course fish
  dinner rooted in the traditional meat abstinence on the vigil of a major feast.
- **Hot cross buns (Good Friday)** — spiced buns marked with a cross, England; the cross both
  marks the day and (by legend) was said to keep the bread from spoiling.
- **Paczki (Fat Tuesday, Polish)** — rich jam-filled donuts made to use up sugar, eggs, and fat
  in the house before Lenten fasting began — same impulse as Mardi Gras elsewhere, different
  pastry.
- **St. Nicholas Day cookies (speculaas/speculoos, Dutch/Belgian)** — spiced cookies, often
  shaped like the saint himself, handed out alongside the shoe-filling custom. (The shoe-filling
  custom and the dowry legend are already published — see `st-nicholas-day-shoes` and
  `nicholas-dowry-gold`.)
- **St. Lucy's Day saffron buns (lussekatter, Scandinavian)** — already noted under feast days;
  worth cross-listing here since the food item is as central as the candle-crown procession.
- **Braided Easter breads (Tsoureki — Greek, Paska/Kulich — Slavic, various others)** — enriched
  sweet breads blessed and eaten at Easter, often dyed eggs baked directly into the braid.
- **St. Joseph's Day fava beans** — already noted under feast days; the "lucky bean" carried in
  a pocket for the rest of the year is a nice small, giftable tradition to highlight.

## Regional patronal festivals (genre piece, not syncretism)

- **Italian *feste*** — village patron saint festivals with processions carrying the saint's
  statue, fireworks, food stalls.
- **Spanish *fiestas patronales*** — similar pattern across Spain and Latin America, strongly
  tied to local identity (a town's patron saint festival is often its biggest annual event).
- **Maltese *festa* tradition** — notably elaborate fireworks and statue processions, a good
  "most intense version of this genre" example.
- **Irish pattern days** ("patron" days) — local pilgrimage/festival days tied to a parish or
  holy well's patron saint.

Frame this whole section as "how Catholic communities localize devotion to a shared saint,"
not as a comparative-religion piece — keeps it inside the Catholic-only guardrail even though
the cultural flavor varies a lot by country.

## Naming traditions

- **Saint's name days** — in much of Europe and Latin America, celebrating the feast day of
  the saint you're named after is traditionally as big as (or bigger than) a birthday.
- **Naming a child after the saint of their birth date** — still a live practice in some
  families/cultures; good short piece on how a baptismal name gets chosen this way.

## Weather / agricultural folklore

- **St. Swithin's Day (Jul 15)** — English legend that rain on this day means 40 more days of
  rain.
- **St. Medard's Day (Jun 8)** — French equivalent of the Swithin legend.

## Traditions born of persecution / concealment

(User flagged interest in how the Church survived persecution — same thread as the new
"Persecution-era legends" section in Pillar 1 and the catacomb-symbol material in Pillar 5. Keep
watching for more candidates here.)

- **Mass rocks (Ireland)** — already published, see `mass-rocks-ireland`. New `traditions`
  category value `persecution-custom` was added to `src/content.config.ts` for this entry and
  the two below.
- **Priest holes (recusant England)** — Catholic households under Elizabethan/Jacobean
  persecution built hidden chambers into manor houses to conceal priests during raids. More
  architecture-and-history than a single legend, but rich "how the faith survived" material.
- **Ring rosaries / "soldier's rosary"** — a ring with ten bumps or knots around the band, so a
  decade of Hail Marys could be prayed discreetly without carrying a visible rosary. Ties
  naturally into the existing liturgical-object section above.
